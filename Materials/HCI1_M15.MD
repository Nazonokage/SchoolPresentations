# User-Centered Design (UCD)

---

## A. Lesson Preview & Review

Welcome to Module 15! In previous modules, we explored how to structure pages and the principles of good visual design. Now, we shift our focus to the most important part of any system: **The User**.

**User-Centered Design (UCD)** is an iterative design process where designers focus on putting users at the center of product design and development. Instead of designing based on what looks cool or what is easiest to code, UCD takes the user's requirements, objectives, and feedback into account in every phase of the process.

> **In a Nutshell:** UCD is about deeply understanding who your users are and what they need, designing a solution for them, and then constantly testing and tweaking it based on their real-world feedback. 

**Learning Objectives:**
By the end of this module, you should be able to:
1. Identify the concepts and principles of User-Centered Design.
2. Understand how to apply a user-centered approach to design.
3. Evaluate a web page based on the 7 key attributes of a user-centered website.

## B. Main Lesson

### 1. The 4 Phases of UCD
UCD is rarely a straight line. It is an **iterative loop**. The design team continuously cycles through these four phases until the evaluation results are satisfactory:

1.  **Understand context of use:** Figure out who the users are, what they are trying to do, and under what conditions they will use the system.
2.  **Specify user requirements:** Identify exactly what the users need the system to do.
3.  **Design solutions:** Create sketches, wireframes, and prototypes based on those requirements.
4.  **Evaluate against requirements:** Test the design with real users to see if it actually solves their problem.

*Note: The design team should include a mix of professionals—ethnographers, psychologists, engineers, domain experts, and the users themselves—using tools like surveys, interviews, and brainstorming.*

### 2. UCD vs. Human-Centered Design (HCD)
These terms are often used interchangeably, but there is a slight difference. UCD is a **subset** of HCD. 
Simply put: *All users are humans, but not all humans will be your users.* UCD requires a deeper analysis of a specific target audience, looking at age, gender, education, and particular habits to solve specific problems.

### 3. The 5 Major UCD Principles
1.  A clear understanding of user and task requirements.
2.  Incorporating user feedback to define requirements and design.
3.  Early and active involvement of the user to evaluate the design.
4.  Integrating user-centered design with other development activities.
5.  An iterative design process.

> **Why is this important?** If you change a design late in the process, it typically costs **ten times more** than if you caught the issue during the requirements stage. Analysis and feedback save time and money.

### 4. Essential Elements of UCD
*   **Visibility:** Users should be able to see immediately what they can do, how to use the product, and what it is about.
*   **Accessibility:** Users should find information easily and quickly using multiple ways (search, menu, call-to-action buttons).
*   **Legibility:** Text should be easy to read (good contrast, proper size).
*   **Language:** Short, clear sentences are preferred. The easier the words, the better.

### 5. Drawbacks of UCD
While highly effective, UCD has potential pitfalls:
*   **Passive user involvement:** Users might not give active, useful feedback.
*   **Inappropriate perception:** Users might have a skewed or incorrect expectation of what the new interface should be.
*   **Wrong questions:** Designers might ask users the wrong questions, leading to bad data.

### 6. The 7 Simple Ways to Achieve a User-Centered Website

How do we apply UCD to web design? Here are seven crucial rules, complete with real-world examples from the module.

#### 1) Concentrate on Visibility
Vital site elements (like navigational aids) must be highly conspicuous. Users should immediately know what they *can* and *cannot* do. 
*   *Example:* **Cisco's** navigation bar is an "ever-present and grabbing" element. No matter where you go on the site, the nav bar remains visible and predictable, creating a feeling of confidence and order.

#### 2) Memory Load Should Be Kept to a Minimum
Nothing is worse than forcing users to remember what a certain icon meant from the previous page. Screen elements must be purposeful and consistent.
*   *Example:* **MSN.com** keeps memory load low by ensuring their navigation bar and site layout remain identical no matter what page you visit.

#### 3) Feedback Ought to Be Instant
Fast feedback tells the user that their actions (like clicking a button) registered and mattered. Something on the site must change after a user action.
*   *Example:* On Microsoft's site, hovering or clicking "products" instantly drops down a menu featuring links. The user doesn't have to wonder if their click worked.

#### 4) Make Accessibility a Priority
Users want information found efficiently and quickly. Accessibility takes the form of good site organization, a search feature, or a sitemap. Content should be broken down into smaller, digestible sections.
*   *Example:* **Apple** organizes their Mac webpage into easy-to-absorb sections (buying, customization, specs) that are highly meaningful to a shopper. 

#### 5) Don't Forget Site Orientation
Users need navigational clues to know where they are relative to other pages. The most powerful examples of this are sitemaps, descriptive links, and highly visible breadcrumbs.
*   *Example:* **PayPal's** sitemap might look like a lot of links, but for a user struggling to find a specific feature, nothing beats a comprehensive sitemap to provide absolute orientation.

#### 6) Make Your Site Pleasant
A pleasant site is simple to use and look at. When users are satisfied with the interface, they gain confidence in the reliability of the information and are motivated to use the site more.
*   *Example:* **Flickr** is a simple repository of photos. Users search, and immediately see endless lists of matching photos. This positive experience acts as positive reinforcement, prompting users to explore more.

#### 7) Tie It Together With Visual Design
Visual design is the aesthetics of the interface. It communicates tone and information. Good visual design mixes interesting elements with simplicity, uses color conservatively, and makes vital elements prominent.
*   *Example:* **Sony** features a homepage that is simple yet eye-catching, with colors used very sparingly to draw the eye to the most important product highlights.

## C. Analogies and Use Cases

### The Car Dashboard Analogy
Think of the 7 principles like driving a car:
*   **Visibility:** You can clearly see the speedometer and fuel gauge without looking away from the road.
*   **Memory Load:** The pedals are always in the same place. You don't have to relearn how to brake every time you get in.
*   **Instant Feedback:** When you press the gas, the car accelerates immediately; the engine doesn't pause for 5 seconds.
*   **Site Orientation:** The GPS map shows you exactly where your car is relative to your destination.

### Modern Application of UCD
In modern tech, UCD is the foundation of **UX (User Experience) Design**. Today, companies use tools like heatmaps (to see where users click) and A/B testing (showing two different designs to different users to see which performs better) to execute the evaluation phase of UCD. Agile software development heavily relies on the "iterative loop" of UCD.

## D. Lesson Wrap-Up / FAQs

### Key Takeaways
*   UCD is an **iterative process** putting the user at the center of design.
*   It requires a multi-disciplinary team and continuous feedback loops.
*   A good user-centered website focuses on **visibility, low memory load, instant feedback, accessibility, orientation, pleasantness, and strong visual design**.
*   Fixing design flaws early saves 10x the cost later.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the difference between UCD and HCD?**
**A:** Human-Centered Design (HCD) focuses broadly on human well-being and capabilities. User-Centered Design (UCD) is a subset of HCD that focuses specifically on the end-users of a particular product or system and their specific tasks.

**Q: Why does UCD sometimes fail?**
**A:** It can fail if user involvement is passive, if designers ask the wrong questions, or if users have an inappropriate perception of what the new interface should be (e.g., they want it to look exactly like their old software even if the old software was terrible).
