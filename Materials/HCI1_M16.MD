## B. Main Lesson

### 1. How Do Users Actually Think?
Before designing, you must understand user behavior. According to usability research (and popularized by experts like Steve Krug), web users behave in very specific ways:

*   **Users appreciate quality and credibility.** If a page provides high-quality content, users will forgive minor design flaws. *Content is more important than the design supporting it.*
*   **Users don't read; they scan.** Users look for fixed points or "anchors" to guide them through the page. They rarely read linearly.
*   **Users are impatient.** They insist on instant gratification. If a site is not intuitive or takes too long to load, they will click "Back" and find an alternative.
*   **Users don't make optimal choices.** They don't search for the *quickest* way; they choose the *first reasonable option* (a behavior called "satisficing").
*   **Users follow their intuition.** They "muddle through" instead of reading instructions. If something works, they stick to it, regardless of how it works under the hood.
*   **Users want to have control.** They rely on their browser's "Back" button. **Never open links in new browser windows unexpectedly**, as it breaks their sense of control and confuses them.

### 2. The 10 Principles of Effective Web Design

Here are the golden rules for building a user-centered website, with modern context added for today's web:

#### 1. Don't Make Users Think (Krug's First Law)
The web page should be **obvious and self-explanatory**. Your job is to get rid of the "question marks"—the decisions users need to make consciously about how to navigate.
*   *Modern Context:* Use clear **microcopy** (small bits of text on buttons or forms) so users don't have to guess. A button saying "Get Started" is better than one saying "Submit."

#### 2. Don't Squander Users' Patience
Keep user requirements minimal. First-time visitors are willing to "play" with a service, but they are not willing to fill out a long registration form just to test it out. Let them explore before forcing them to share private data.
*   *Modern Context:* Ever notice how e-commerce sites now offer **"Guest Checkout"**? That is a direct application of this principle. Forcing account creation before purchase causes massive cart abandonment.

#### 3. Manage To Focus Users' Attention
Websites provide both static and dynamic content. Images catch the eye more than text, and bold text catches the eye more than plain text. You must guide the user's focus to the most important elements.
*   *Example:* The "Humanized" website focuses entirely on the word **"FREE"** which acts as an attractive, purely informative anchor for the user.

#### 4. Strive For Feature Exposure
Guiding users with visually appealing "1-2-3 done" steps and large buttons with visual effects is not a bad thing. These elements are effective because they lead visitors through the site content in a simple, user-friendly way.

#### 5. Make Use of Effective Writing
The web is not print. Long text blocks without images will be skipped. Exaggerated marketing language will be ignored. 
*   **Talk business:** Avoid cute, clever, or unfamiliar technical names. If you want users to create an account, use plain language like "Sign up" rather than "Start now!" or "Explore our services."
*   Use short, concise phrases, multiple heading levels, and bulleted lists.

#### 6. Strive For Simplicity (KIS Principle)
"Keep It Simple" should be your primary goal. From the visitor's point of view, the best site design is a pure text layout without distracting advertisements or unnecessary content blocks.

#### 7. Don't Be Afraid of the White Space
It is hard to overestimate the importance of white (or negative) space. It reduces cognitive load and makes it easier to digest information. 
*   **Simon's Law:** Hierarchical structures reduce complexity. If you have a choice, separate design segments with whitespace rather than visible lines. It makes the content easier to perceive.

#### 8. Communicate Effectively With a "Visible Language"
Aaron Marcus states there are three fundamental principles of visual communication on screen:
*   **Organize:** Provide a clear and consistent conceptual structure (consistency, screen layout).
*   **Economize:** Do the most with the least amount of cues (simplicity, clarity, distinctiveness, emphasis).
*   **Communicate:** Match the presentation to the user's capabilities. Use a maximum of **3 typefaces** in a maximum of **3 point sizes**. Keep lines to a maximum of 18 words or 50-80 characters.

#### 9. Conventions Are Our Friends
Conventional design does not result in a boring website; it results in an intuitive one. Conventions reduce the learning curve. Users expect certain things (like the shopping cart icon in the top right, or the logo in the top left). 
*   *Modern Context:* The "Hamburger Menu" (three horizontal lines) is a modern convention. You don't need to explain it; users already know what it does.

#### 10. Test Early, Test Often (TETO)
Usability tests provide crucial insights into problems with your layout. 
*   **Steve Krug:** Testing one user is 100% better than testing none. Testing one user early is better than testing 50 near the end.
*   **Boehm's First Law:** Errors are most frequent during requirements and design activities and are more expensive the later they are removed.
*   **Weinberg's Law:** A developer is unsuited to test their own code. Designers suffer from the "curse of knowledge"—you know how the site works, so you can't see it from a fresh perspective anymore.

## C. Analogies and Use Cases

### The Restaurant Soft Opening Analogy (TETO)
Imagine opening a restaurant. You don't wait until opening night with 500 reservations to see if the kitchen works. You do a "soft opening" with a few friends and family members (testing one user) to see if the menu makes sense, if the kitchen is too slow, and if the layout of the dining room is comfortable. Testing early saves you from a disaster on opening night.

### Use Case: The Power of Conventions
Imagine if every time you got into a new car, the steering wheel was in a different place, the gas pedal was on the left, and the brake was on the ceiling. You would crash! Websites are the same. Because the "search bar" or "shopping cart" is always in the same place, we don't have to relearn the internet every day. **Conventions make the web usable.**

## D. Lesson Wrap-Up / FAQs

### Key Takeaways
*   Users **scan**, they don't read. Keep it simple.
*   Don't make users think; use **conventions** and clear layouts.
*   Respect users' patience by minimizing required actions (like forcing logins).
*   **White space** and **hierarchy** reduce cognitive load.
*   **TETO (Test Early, Test Often)**: Designers cannot objectively test their own work. Get real users involved immediately.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What does "Cognitive Load" mean?**
**A:** It's the amount of mental effort required to use a system. If a site has too many colors, confusing menus, and long paragraphs, the cognitive load is high, and users will leave. White space and simplicity reduce cognitive load.

**Q: Why is testing early so important?**
**A:** Because fixing a mistake in the design phase (a sketch or wireframe) takes minutes. Fixing a mistake in the code phase takes days or weeks and costs a lot of money.
