## A. Lesson Preview & Review

Welcome to the Capstone of your HCI journey! Over the past modules, you have learned about user-centered design, usability principles, responsive layouts, and the basics of HTML. Now, it is time to put all that knowledge into practice.

In this final project, you will work in groups (2-3 members) to plan, design, and build a website. You will act as both the UX/UI designer and the front-end developer, applying HCI concepts to ensure your final product is not just visually pleasing, but highly usable and accessible.

> **In a Nutshell:** You are going to experience the real-world software development lifecycle—from pitching an idea (Proposal) to drawing it out (Wireframes/Mockups), writing the code (Coding), and finally getting feedback (Evaluation).

**Learning Objectives:**
By the end of this module, you should be able to:
1. Identify the steps and procedures for developing a web page from concept to code.
2. Understand the importance of planning, prototyping, and evaluating a web page.
3. Create a proposal, design plan, functional prototype, and final coded website that applies HCI concepts.

## B. Main Lesson: The 6 Phases of Final Coursework

Here is your roadmap for the final project. You will move through these phases systematically.

### Phase 1: Proposal (Ideation)
Every great website starts with a clear idea and purpose.
*   **What to do:** Propose a sample webpage you want to create.
*   **Requirements:**
    *   Title/Name of the page.
    *   List of possible content (What text/images will be on it?).
    *   List of possible features (e.g., a search bar, a contact form, an image slider).
    *   **HCI Application:** Explain how HCI concepts (like User-Centered Design, Usability, or Accessibility) will apply to your webpage.

> **Modern Addendum:** In professional practice, a proposal is often accompanied by a **User Persona** (a fictional character representing your ideal user) and a **Statement of Purpose** (e.g., "This site will help college students find affordable textbooks").

### Phase 2: Sketching, Wireframes, Storyboard, and Mockups (Design)
Now, visualize your idea. You can choose to subscribe primarily to User-Centered or Task-Centered design principles.
*   **What to do:** Create the visual assets of your proposed output.
*   **Requirements:**
    *   **Sketch:** Rough, hand-drawn ideas of the content.
    *   **Wireframes:** Low-fidelity structural outlines (like blueprints).
    *   **Storyboard:** A sequential visual narrative of how a user navigates the features.
    *   **Mockups:** High-fidelity visual designs (colors, real text, images).

> **Modern Addendum:** While hand-drawing is great for the sketch phase, you should use digital tools for wireframes and mockups. **Figma**, **Adobe XD**, or **Balsamiq** are the industry standards for this phase.

### Phase 3: Target Presentation (Pitching)
It's time to present your plan and design for initial feedback.
*   **What to do:** Present your plan and design to your instructor/classmates.
*   **Requirements:**
    *   Complete list of features and content.
    *   Complete mock design of the website.
*   **The Goal:** To get early feedback. Revisions are much easier to make at this stage than after you've written a single line of code.

### Phase 4: Coding and Content (Development)
Time to build! Follow the designs you created in Phase 2.
*   **What to do:** Start coding your website.
*   **Requirements:** The HTML file and related files (images, audio, CSS).
*   **HCI Focus:** Remember to use semantic HTML (correct heading structure), add `alt` tags to images for accessibility, and include the viewport meta tag for responsiveness.

> **Modern Addendum:** While this module focuses on HTML, a real-world site requires CSS for styling and JavaScript for interactivity. Use a code editor like **VS Code** and consider using **Git** for version control so you don't lose your progress.

### Phase 5: Prototypes (Mid-Point Check)
You don't need a 100% finished site to get feedback.
*   **What to do:** Present your current progress.
*   **Requirements:**
    *   A working prototype of the proposed project.
    *   More than 50% completion.
*   **The Goal:** To ensure you are on the right track and to identify major technical or design roadblocks.

### Phase 6: Evaluation (Feedback & Iteration)
The final step in the iterative design process.
*   **What to do:** Let other groups evaluate your project. Consider their feedback for improving your website. The teacher will also give feedback.
*   **Requirements:**
    *   Current progress rating from the other group (peer review).
    *   Current progress rating from your teacher.

> **Modern Addendum:** In the real world, this is called **Usability Testing**. You sit a user down in front of your site, give them a task (e.g., "Find the contact page"), and watch what they do without helping them. Their struggles are your most valuable data.

## C. Analogies and Use Cases

### The Construction Analogy
*   **Phase 1 (Proposal):** The client says, "I want a 3-bedroom house with a big kitchen."
*   **Phase 2 (Design):** The architect draws the blueprints (wireframes) and creates a 3D rendering (mockup).
*   **Phase 4 (Coding):** The construction crew pours the foundation and builds the walls (HTML) and paints them (CSS).
*   **Phase 6 (Evaluation):** The homeowner does a walkthrough. They realize a light switch is in a weird place. The electrician moves it *before* the final handover.

### Use Case: Why Phase 3 is Crucial
Imagine spending 20 hours coding a complex navigation menu. During Phase 6 evaluation, you realize users find the menu confusing and prefer a search bar instead. You've wasted 20 hours. If you had presented that menu in Phase 3, you could have caught the flaw after spending only 1 hour drawing it in Figma.

## D. Lesson Wrap-Up / FAQs

### Key Takeaways
*   **Planning prevents poor performance.** The design phases (1-3) are just as important as the coding phase.
*   **Iteration is key.** Presenting your work at Phase 3 and Phase 5 allows you to fix mistakes before the final submission.
*   **Don't code in a vacuum.** Always refer back to your wireframes and mockups.
*   **HCI is practical.** You are being graded on how well you apply usability and accessibility principles to your actual code and design.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the most difficult phase?**
**A:** Many students find **Phase 4 (Coding)** the most technically challenging. It's easy to draw a button, but harder to make it actually link to another page. *Tip:* Start coding early and test in your browser frequently.

**Q: What if my group members aren't doing their part?**
**A:** Divide the work clearly during Phase 1. One person can be the Lead Designer (Phase 2), one the Lead Coder (Phase 4), and one the QA/Project Manager. Use tools like Google Docs or Trello to track who is doing what.

**Q: Do I need to code a fully functional backend (database)?**
**A:** Based on the module, you only need the HTML file and related media files (frontend). You are building the *interface*, not necessarily a working login system (unless your instructor specifies otherwise).
