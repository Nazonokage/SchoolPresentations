## B. Main Lesson

### Part 1: The 7 Elements of Design
The elements of design are the fundamental aspects of any visual design. Graphic designers use these to create images that convey a certain mood, draw the eye in a specific direction, or evoke feelings. 

**1. Color**
Color helps establish a **mood** for your composition. It is the sensation perceived when light waves strike an object and reflect back to the optic nerve. Designers use the color wheel and color theory to create schemes that depict mood, light, depth, and point of view.
*   *Example:* Blue often conveys trust and calm, while red conveys urgency or energy.

**2. Line**
A line refers to the way **two points in space are connected**. Whether horizontal, diagonal, or vertical, lines help direct the viewer's eye toward a certain point in your composition. You can also use curved or patterned lines to create texture.

**3. Value**
Value refers to the **lightness or darkness** of a color. Values are often visualized in a gradient, arranged from lightest to darkest. Artists use value to create the illusion of mass, volume, and depth in a two-dimensional space.

**4. Space**
Making proper use of space helps others view your design as intended. 
*   **Negative space (White space):** The space between or around the focal point of an image.
*   **Positive space:** The space that your subject matter takes up.
*   *Rule of thumb:* A layout that is too crowded can overwhelm the viewer's eye.

**5. Shape**
A shape is a **two-dimensional area surrounded by an outline**. There are three main types:
*   **Organic shapes:** Occur naturally in the world (e.g., a leaf, a cloud).
*   **Geometric shapes:** Angular and mathematically consistent (e.g., squares, triangles).
*   **Abstract shapes:** Represent things in nature but aren't perfectly representative (e.g., a stylized icon of a human).

**6. Form**
Form pertains to the way a shape or physical configuration **occupies space**. While shape is 2D, form is the illusion of 3D on a flat surface. Designers create the appearance of form using light, shadow, contours, and negative space.

**7. Texture**
Texture represents how an object **appears or feels**. 
*   **Tactile texture:** Physical sense of touch (rough, smooth, ribbed).
*   **Visual texture:** The *imagined* feel of an illustrated texture, which can create visual interest and a heightened sensory experience.

### Part 2: Sketches vs. Wireframes

Before you jump into digital design software, you need to plan. This is done through sketching and wireframing.

#### Sketches
The intent of sketching is to separate design from the process of making. Sketching is rapid, freehand drawing done with **no intention of it becoming a finished product**. It is the foundation upon which you overlay your actual design work. 

**Characteristics of a Good Sketch:**
*   **Quick, timely, and inexpensive:** Uses materials you have on hand.
*   **Disposable and plentiful:** You don't care if they get thrown away; they don't exist in isolation.
*   **Fluid and clear:** Uses simple symbols and lines.
*   **Minimally detailed and ambiguous:** Sketches are conceptual and exploratory. They suggest structure without confirming concrete decisions.

#### Wireframes
A wireframe is a **basic visual guide** used to suggest the structure of a website and the relationships between its pages. It comes *after* sketching and before visual design. It is a "thinking device" used to explore concepts and outline the basic flow.

**Characteristics of a Good Wireframe:**
*   **Viable:** You've narrowed down your concepts to the best options (they are not as plentiful as sketches).
*   **Confirmatory:** They present concepts that need validation.
*   **Appropriately refined:** They communicate a clear sense of structure and layout using simple symbols and indicators of interactivity.

### Part 3: The 7-Step Wireframe Creation Process

**1. Identify the goal of the website.**
What do you want visitors to do? Buy a product? Download an app? Read an article? Make sure your team is aligned on this goal before you start.

**2. Understand the user flow.**
Outline how a visitor will interact with each page. Start with primary entry points (like the homepage) and map out the journey. *Tip: Outline the user flow in a text document first—it's faster to move steps around that way.*

**3. Determine your website wireframe size.**
Use pixel measurements rather than inches. Standard sizes include:
| Device | Standard Size |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Mobile** | 1080px wide x 1920px long |
| **8" Tablet** | 800px wide x 1280px long |
| **10" Tablet** | 1200px wide x 1920px long |
| **Desktop** | 768px wide x 1366px long |

**4. Begin your website wireframe design.**
Visualize your user flow. If using pen and paper, use dotted or grid paper for alignment. If going digital, choose a tool that fits your fidelity needs (low, medium, or high).

**5. Determine conversion points.**
Decide exactly how the user will move to the next step. Determine what buttons, hyperlinks, or images will guide the reader toward the end goal.

**6. Remove redundant steps.**
Wireframing is iterative. Look for opportunities to simplify the process and create fewer clicks for the user. 

**7. Get feedback on the wireframe.**
Collaborate with design teams, developers, and internal staff to get their opinions on the flow before adding final buttons or layouts.

## C. Analogies and Use Cases

### The Architect Analogy
Think of **Sketches** as a napkin drawing an architect makes when brainstorming a house layout with a client. It's messy, fast, and changes rapidly. Think of **Wireframes** as the blueprints—they are precise, show where the doors and windows go, and serve as the guide for the builders. You wouldn't hand a napkin drawing to a construction crew!

### Use Case: E-Commerce Checkout
When designing the checkout flow for an online store:
*   **Goal:** The user completes a purchase.
*   **Wireframe:** The designer creates a low-fidelity wireframe of the cart, shipping, and payment pages. 
*   **Conversion Point:** A big, clear "Proceed to Payment" button.
*   **Redundant Step Removal:** Instead of making the user create an account, add a "Guest Checkout" button to reduce friction.

## D. Lesson Wrap-Up / FAQs

### Key Takeaways
*   **Elements of Design** (Color, Line, Value, Space, Shape, Form, Texture) are the building blocks of visual composition.
*   **Sketches** are quick, messy, disposable brainstorming tools.
*   **Wireframes** are structured, basic visual guides that define the layout and user flow of a website.
*   A good wireframe process starts with understanding the **user's goal** and ends with **feedback** from the team.

### Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the difference between Shape and Form?**
**A:** Shape is strictly 2D (height and width). Form is 3D (height, width, and depth). On a screen, designers use shadows and lighting to give a 2D shape the illusion of 3D form.

**Q: Why is negative space important?**
**A:** Negative space (white space) gives the viewer's eye a rest. It helps group elements together and makes important elements pop. Without it, a design looks cluttered and overwhelming.

**Q: Can I just skip sketching and go straight to wireframing?**
**A:** You *can*, but it's not recommended. Sketching allows you to brainstorm wildly without worrying about perfection. It helps you generate many ideas quickly before committing to a structured wireframe.
