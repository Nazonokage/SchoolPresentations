## B. Main Lesson

### What is a Security Policy?
You may not have noticed, but simple instructions like *"Log out of your account after you are done using the work computer"* or *"Wear your ID at all times"* are actually **Security Policies**. 

A security policy is a document containing rules and regulations within an organization. Its primary objective is to protect the organization's physical and IT assets, its employees, and its customers. 

**How Logging Out and Wearing IDs Protect an Organization:**
*   **Logging Out:** Ensures no traces of your activity remain. If the computer is accidentally opened, no one else can access your data.
*   **Wearing IDs:** Helps security guards and employees identify who belongs in the workplace. People without IDs are treated as suspicious or unauthorized.

> **Pro Tip:** Security policies often look ordinary and easy to follow. That is their beauty—they are simple, actionable, and highly effective.

#### In-Depth Security Policies
A formal security policy document is created by security officers, read by every employee, and **approved by executives** (similar to the Authorization step in the RMF). 

A comprehensive policy document contains:
*   Objectives/Purpose
*   Scope
*   The Policy Itself
*   Technical Guidelines
*   Reporting Guidelines/Requirements

For this course, we will focus on creating simple, straightforward policies that anyone in an organization can follow.

### Understanding User Access Control (UAC)
**User Access Control** is the practice of limiting users on how they can change, delete, update, create, or view things in a document or any part of the system. This reduces or prevents damages and unauthorized alterations.

#### UAC Levels
Here are the standard levels of User Access Control, ranging from the highest administrative level to the lowest customer level:

| Level | Title | Permissions |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Level 4** | **Creator / Admin (Highest)** | Can create, input, edit, comment, view, and modify the whole system. Can add other users to different UAC levels. |
| **Level 3** | **High Employee** | Depends on the access rights given. Usually can input, edit, comment, view, and sometimes add other users depending on rights given. |
| **Level 2** | **Employee** | Can edit, comment, and view. |
| **Level 1** | **Customer (Lowest)** | Can view, and sometimes comment. |

> **Important Note:** User Access Controls differ for every organization and situation. A small business might only need 3 levels, while a large enterprise might need 5 or more.

#### UAC Example: Fast Food Restaurant Menu Pricing Display
Let's apply the UAC levels to a real-world scenario:
*   **Level 4 (Owners):** Can create menus to be displayed and provide basic pricing.
*   **Level 3 (Managers):** Can modify the displayed menu and modify the pricing.
*   **Level 2 (Employees/Staff):** Can modify the displayed menu depending on the availability of items.
*   **Level 1 (Customers):** Can view the menu and its pricing.

## C. Analogies and Use Cases

### The "Hotel Keycard" Analogy
Think of User Access Control as a hotel keycard system.
*   **Level 4 (General Manager):** Has a master key that opens every room, the maintenance closet, and the accounting office.
*   **Level 3 (Housekeeping Supervisor):** Can open guest rooms and the supply closet, but cannot access the accounting office.
*   **Level 2 (Housekeeper):** Can only open guest rooms to clean them.
*   **Level 1 (Guest):** Can only open their specific room and the front lobby. They can't open other guests' rooms or staff areas.

### Real-World Use Case: A University Grades Portal
Imagine a university's online grading system.
*   **Level 4 (System Admin):** Can create new semesters, enroll students, and modify the database structure.
*   **Level 3 (Registrar/Dean):** Can view all grades, approve grade changes, and generate reports.
*   **Level 2 (Teacher):** Can input and edit grades *only* for the specific subjects they are assigned to. They cannot see the grades of subjects they don't teach.
*   **Level 1 (Student):** Can only view their own grades. They cannot modify them or see anyone else's grades.

## D. Lesson Wrap-Up / FAQs

### Key Takeaways
*   **Security Policies** are formal documents that dictate rules and regulations to protect an organization's assets. They must be approved by executives.
*   Policies vary widely by industry, but they all aim to create a safe and secure environment.
*   **User Access Control (UAC)** restricts what users can do within a system based on their role.
*   UAC levels typically range from Level 1 (View-only) to Level 4 (Admin/Creator), ensuring the principle of least privilege.
*   UAC must be applied to every aspect of an organization's system and documentation.

### Frequently Asked Questions
**1. Do all security policies look simple?**
**A:** Not all. In higher levels of an organization, some security policies are complex and hard for some people to understand. For example, policies regarding software development standards or data encryption protocols require specialized knowledge. However, the most effective policies for general staff are often the simplest ones.

**2. Why is it important to have User Access Controls?**
**A:** UAC is crucial for preventing accidental or malicious damage. By limiting who can edit, delete, or view sensitive information, an organization reduces the risk of data breaches, unauthorized changes, and internal errors.

**3. How do I decide which UAC level to assign to an employee?**
**A:** Always follow the **Principle of Least Privilege**. Assign the lowest level of access necessary for the employee to perform their job duties. If they need more access later, it can be granted on a case-by-case basis.

**4. Can a security policy change over time?**
**A:** Yes. Security policies must be reviewed and updated regularly to address new threats, changes in technology, and shifts in organizational structure.
