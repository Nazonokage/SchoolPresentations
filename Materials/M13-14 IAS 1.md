## B. Main Lesson

### Security Control Types
Security controls are the safeguards or countermeasures prescribed for an information system to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of the system and its information. There are three primary types:

**1. Physical Security**
Physical security relies on the use of **physical limitations** that prevent a criminal or unauthorized person from accessing assets. These controls are **tangible**—meaning you can touch or hold them.
*   *Examples:* Doors, Locks, Gates, CCTV cameras, or repairing/fixing broken infrastructure.

**2. Technical Security**
Technical security refers to the **intangible tools** used to secure assets. These are mainly software solutions installed on the computers and networks within an organization.
*   *Examples:* Firewalls, Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS), Patching a system to update it, and robust password checking systems.

**3. Administrative Security**
Administrative security encompasses the policies, procedures, and guidelines used to enforce the attitude and ethics of the organization. These are often documentation-based and work alongside physical and technical security to ensure overall safety.
*   *Examples:* Security Policies, Access Rights, Risk management plans, and proper Password making policies.

### Security Control Functions
Now that we understand the *types* of controls, let's look at them from a different perspective: their **functions**. How do these controls actually protect us?

*   **Preventive Controls:** Designed to **stop** unwanted or unauthorized activity from occurring. In other words, to prevent bad things from happening before they start.
*   **Detective Controls:** Implemented to **detect and alert** to unwanted or unauthorized activity in progress or after it has occurred. These look for ongoing or recently occurred illegal activity within your system.
*   **Corrective Controls:** Measures taken to **repair damage or restore** resources and capabilities to their prior state following an unauthorized or unwanted activity. In other words, to fix what was broken and make it function better.

### Combining Control Types and Functions
A good security strategy doesn't just use one type or one function. It combines both. Here is a matrix illustrating how they work together:

| | Preventive | Detective | Corrective |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Physical** | Fences, Gates, Locks | CCTV | Repair Gates |
| **Technical** | Firewalls | Intrusion Detection Systems | Patching to fix zero-day exploits |
| **Administrative** | Hiring Policies | Checking User Access Rights Records | Disaster Recovery Plan |

**Examples in Action:**
1.  A **CCTV** is a **Physical** Security Control using a **Detective** Control Function. (It watches for intruders).
2.  An **Intrusion Detection System** is a **Technical** Security Control using a **Detective** Control Function. (It alerts admins of network breaches).
3.  A **Gate** is a **Physical** Security Control using a **Preventive** Control Function. (It physically blocks entry).
4.  A **Disaster Recovery Plan** is an **Administrative** Security Control using a **Corrective** Control Function. (It restores operations after a disaster).

## C. Analogies and Use Cases

### The "Fortress" Analogy
Imagine you are protecting a medieval fortress.
*   **Physical (Preventive):** The high stone walls and the moat. They physically stop invaders.
*   **Physical (Detective):** The guards patrolling the walls with torches looking for shadows. They spot the enemy.
*   **Physical (Corrective):** The masons who repair the wall after a catapult hits it.
*   **Administrative (Preventive):** The King's law that states "Anyone caught outside after dark will be arrested." (Policy).
*   **Technical (Detective):** A magical alarm spell that rings when someone touches the gate. (IDS).

### Real-World Use Case: A Remote Workforce
A company employs remote workers.
*   **Administrative (Preventive):** The company creates a "Work From Home Policy" requiring employees to use company-issued laptops and VPNs.
*   **Technical (Preventive):** The IT team installs a firewall on the company laptops.
*   **Technical (Detective):** The IT team sets up an IDS to monitor for unusual login times or locations.
*   **Administrative (Corrective):** If an employee's laptop is stolen, they trigger the "Incident Response Plan," remotely wiping the device and issuing a new one.

## D. Lesson Wrap-Up / FAQs

### Key Takeaways
*   **Control Types** describe the *nature* of the control: Physical (tangible), Technical (software/hardware), or Administrative (policies).
*   **Control Functions** describe the *purpose* of the control: Preventive (stops), Detective (finds), or Corrective (fixes).
*   The most effective security strategies combine multiple types and functions. 
*   **Administrative controls** (like policies) are just as important as physical and technical controls.

### Frequently Asked Questions
**1. Will we be able to cover every security control type and function in our risk assessment?**
**A:** No, it's rare to cover every single combination perfectly. But remember that the RMF is a cycle. You will continuously add control types and functions as you identify new risks and adjust your framework.

**2. What is the difference between a "Control Type" and a "Control Function"?**
**A:** A **Control Type** tells you *what the control is made of* (e.g., a physical lock, a software program, a written policy). A **Control Function** tells you *what the control does* (e.g., it prevents access, it detects an intrusion, it corrects a vulnerability).

**3. Can a single control serve multiple functions?**
**A:** Yes! For example, a **Security Guard** (Physical Type) can be Preventive (standing at the door), Detective (patrolling the halls), and Corrective (escorting an intruder out). However, it is often better to have specialized controls for each function.

**4. Why are Administrative Controls necessary if we have Firewalls and Locks?**
**A:** Because technology cannot fix human error. Administrative controls (like training and policies) ensure that employees know how to use the technical and physical controls correctly. For example, a lock is useless if an employee holds the door open for a stranger.
