## B. Main Lesson

### Implementing Security Controls (Step 3)
In the previous step, we selected our security controls based on their type and function. Now, we must **implement** them within the organization. How do we actually do this?

Implementation depends on the type of control and the organization's internal processes. But in every scenario, **documentation** is the critical component.

| Control Type | How it is Implemented | Required Documentation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Physical** | Hiring a security guard, installing locks, building fences. | Request forms, purchase orders, installation logs. |
| **Administrative** | Updating security policies, conducting training. | Updated policy manuals, employee training records, memos. |
| **Technical** | Buying software, configuring firewalls, patching systems. | IT job tickets, software licenses, configuration logs. |

> **Pro Tip:** As a security officer, your role is to **recommend**. The organization then enacts these recommendations. In your documentation, you must clearly state the risk, the recommended control type and function, and the specific solution.

#### Writing Recommendations
When documenting your recommendations, you can use either an enumerated list or a paragraph format. Here are examples of both:

**Example 1: Enumerated Recommendation**
*   **Risk:** Physical theft of office equipment or assets.
*   **Security Control Type and Function:** Preventive Physical Security and Detective Physical Security.
*   **Recommendation:** It is recommended to hire a security guard to check and protect the premises. Additionally, equip the rooms and hallways with CCTV cameras to detect intruders.

**Example 2: Paragraph Recommendation**
*   **Risk:** Flooding.
*   **Recommendation:** On the risk of flooding, we recommend a **Preventive Administrative Security** control. This involves creating a policy that mandates the transfer of all water-vulnerable assets to a higher ground to prevent destruction from floods.

### Assessing Security Controls (Step 4)
Once a control is implemented, it must be assessed. This step is typically performed by the organization's internal security officers or an assigned employee. As a student, you are not hired to perform this assessment, but your job is to **create the framework** for them to do so.

The assessor's job is to answer three fundamental questions about each recommendation:

**Question 1: Is the recommendation enough, or do we need to apply something more to improve it?**
To answer this, the assessor must study the recommendation and hypothesize what would happen if the risk occurred. If the implemented control was not enough to prevent, detect, or correct what occurred, then the recommendation must be adjusted or increased.

**Question 2: Is there a cheaper option for our recommendation?**
This is often the most important question asked by executives. Security officers must convince business owners that their solution is the cheapest and best decision for the risk. For example, sometimes a CCTV system is more cost-effective and sufficient compared to hiring a full-time security guard.

**Question 3: Is the risk completely correct, or does it need configuration?**
This is answered by selecting one of the four **Risk Treatment Options**:

| Risk Treatment Option | Description | Goal | Frequency |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Avoid the Risk** | Eliminating the risk entirely by avoiding the activity that causes it. | 100% elimination. | Rare (only works for 1/3 of all risks). |
| **Reduce the Risk** | Mitigating the damage the risk can cause through the correct security control. | Reduce likelihood or impact. | Most common option. |
| **Transfer the Risk** | Shifting the risk to a third party, such as an insurance company or a parent organization. | Share the burden of damage. | Rare (requires a 3rd party). |
| **Accept the Risk** | Acknowledging the risk and taking no action because the potential damage is too small. | Monitor only. | Least recommended (worst option). |

By the end of the assessment, you will have answered these three questions and determined the best path forward.

## C. Analogies and Use Cases

### The "Home Renovation" Analogy
Think of implementing and assessing security controls like renovating a house.
*   **Implementation (Step 3):** You decide to install a new alarm system. You hire an electrician, buy the equipment, and document the installation in your home records.
*   **Assessment (Step 4):** After the installation, you test the alarm. 
    1.  *Is it enough?* Yes, but you realize the windows are still weak, so you add window sensors (improve it).
    2.  *Is there a cheaper option?* Instead of a $200/month monitoring service, you buy a smart alarm that alerts your phone for free (cheaper option).
    3.  *Does it need configuration?* You realize the dog might trigger the motion sensor. You configure it to "pet-friendly" mode (Risk Treatment: Reduce).

### Real-World Use Case: A Hospital Data Breach
A hospital identifies a risk of patient data being stolen.
*   **Recommendation:** Implement a **Technical / Preventive** control by purchasing new encryption software for patient databases.
*   **Assessment:** The IT manager assesses this. The software costs $50,000. 
    *   *Is it enough?* Yes, but they also need to train staff on how to use it (Administrative control added).
    *   *Is there a cheaper option?* The vendor offers a cloud-based version for $10,000 annually, which is cheaper upfront.
    *   *Risk Treatment:* They decide to **Reduce** the risk by using the cloud encryption software and transferring the remaining risk to a cybersecurity insurance policy.

## D. Lesson Wrap-Up / FAQs

### Key Takeaways
*   **Step 3 (Implement):** Requires documentation (requests, policies, tickets) to put controls into action. Security officers recommend, executives approve.
*   **Step 4 (Assess):** Involves verifying if the control is adequate, cost-effective, and correctly configured.
*   The **3 Assessment Questions** guide the assessor in recommending improvements, finding cheaper alternatives, and applying the correct risk treatment.
*   The four **Risk Treatment Options** are: Avoid, Reduce, Transfer, and Accept (with Reduce being the most common).

### Frequently Asked Questions
**1. Who is responsible for assessing security controls?**
**A:** In a real-world setting, this is usually the manager or executive assigned to that work department. They are the ones who monitor the risk and are directly responsible for the assets.

**2. Why is "Accept the Risk" considered the worst option?**
**A:** Accepting the risk means the organization acknowledges the danger but does nothing to prevent or mitigate it. If the risk materializes, the organization will suffer the full brunt of the damage. It is only recommended when the potential damage is truly negligible.

**3. Can we implement a control without documentation?**
**A:** No. In a professional environment, undocumented controls are impossible to track, audit, or assess. Documentation ensures accountability and helps future security officers understand why a control was chosen.

**4. What if the cheapest option is not the best?**
**A:** The goal is not just to find the cheapest option, but the *best value* for the organization. The recommended solution must be both effective and cost-efficient. If a cheaper option leaves a critical vulnerability open, it is not a good recommendation.

*Identify the correct Risk Treatment Option (Avoid, Reduce, Transfer, Accept) based on the scenario.*

1.  A company decides to stop using a specific third-party software because it has too many security flaws.
    **Answer: Avoid the Risk**
2.  A hospital buys cybersecurity insurance to cover potential financial losses from a data breach.
    **Answer: Transfer the Risk**
3.  A school installs a fence around its perimeter to prevent unauthorized entry.
    **Answer: Reduce the Risk**

    **Answer: Accept the Risk**
