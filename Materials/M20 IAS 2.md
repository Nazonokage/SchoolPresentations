## B. Main Lesson

### What is Social Engineering?
**Social Engineering** is the process of using social skills to convince people to reveal access credentials or other valuable information to the attacker. This is commonly known as scamming. Sadly, this is still very effective in the IT industry, where thousands of people are scammed every day because they think they can trust the person they are talking to.

Social engineering can be done in **physical/actual conversations** or **virtual conversations**. You might be in a bar talking to a stranger, little knowing that the information you are giving is actually useful to them—or worse, it was their plan all along to get information from you to hack your company's system.

Normally, a hacker looks for a vulnerability in a system. But for social engineers, they could pose as a technical support person to trick an employee into giving their login credentials. Usually, it comes down to how the social engineer becomes appealing to the victim, gaining their trust. Social engineering mostly uses research for their mode of operation, which makes them far more dangerous than other types of scams or hackers.

### Common Types of Social Engineering Attacks

| Attack Type | Description | Examples |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Customer Support Scam** | Posing as technical or customer support for common people posting problems on the internet. | An attacker sees a tweet complaining about `GCash`, creates an account named "GCash Support," and messages the user. They earn trust, ask for credentials to "fix" the issue, then drain the wallet. Also applies to Lazada, eBay, Amazon, and Shopee. |
| **Credit Card / Bank Info Theft** | Sending emails to people whose email addresses are publicly visible, posing as a trusted entity. | Scammers email schools, business owners, or directors offering "new books, technologies, or methods." They ask for payment, decline it, and ask for credentials to "fix" it, stealing bank/credit card credentials. |
| **Tricking Seniors** | Preying on the gullibility of the elderly, especially those who are not tech-savvy. | Scammers send emails posing as the victim's **grandchildren** saying they need money. Or they pose as "customer support" saying the senior's computer is hacked. |
| **DeepFakes** | Using Artificial Intelligence to generate fake images and videos of real people. | Scammers can impersonate famous people or create a virtual mask of someone you know (using public photos from Facebook) to trick your relatives into giving information. |
| **Kidnapping / Physical Harm** | Using social engineering to track physical movements for kidnapping or human trafficking. | Parents freely post about their children. Scammers analyze this data to predict the child's route to school or home. Over-sharing on social media makes it easy to know where someone is at a specific time. |

> **Pro Tip:** DeepFakes are a danger to our current technological age. Facial recognition technology is not yet strong enough to detect them, but facial *copying* technology is already advanced. Be mindful of what you post publicly.

### Protection Against Social Engineering
We finally got to the part where we protect ourselves. Here are four crucial rules to follow:

1.  **Never share your credentials.** 
    Even banks never ask for your PINs. Online stores and websites never ask for your login information. The ones who ask are always scammers.
2.  **Think and be smart.**
    This might be obvious, but for everything you do—such as posting online or sharing posts—always think if it will compromise your safety. If it even slightly compromises your safety, that is more than enough for the red alert in your head to activate. Take your time to think before posting or sharing information.
3.  **Secure your account.**
    Make sure your account can never be seen publicly. If you must be public (e.g., you are a celebrity), only share information that everyone already knows. Never share your personal email address or physical address. A good way to test this is to search your name in Google. If it comes out meaning your account is public, it is not private.
4.  **Research before you click.**
    Sometimes you can receive an email or message online trying to "help" with your problems. Simply search the sender's name on the internet and check if they are a scammer. If it is a new sender, go back to rule 2.

## C. Analogies and Use Cases

### The "Trojan Horse" Analogy
Think of social engineering as a modern-day Trojan Horse. The attackers don't break down the city walls (hack the firewall). Instead, they present themselves as a gift (a helpful customer support agent, a friend in need, a grandchild in trouble). The victims willingly open the gates and invite the threat inside.

### Real-World Use Case: The Twitter Support Scam
A friend ranted on Twitter about `GCash` being unresponsive. They used the hashtag `#GCashProblems`, making the post easy to find. An attacker created an account named "GCash Support" and messaged them, asking for details about the issue. They earned their trust, and finally "found" a solution, but it required the user to give their credentials. Once the attacker had the credentials, they simply took the money inside the GCash wallet. This is a typical social engineering act that happens globally, with scammers posing as customer support for online stores like Lazada, eBay, Amazon, and Shopee.

## D. Lesson Wrap-Up / FAQs

### Key Takeaways
*   **Social Engineering** exploits human psychology and trust, not just technical vulnerabilities.
*   **Common attacks** include fake customer support, bank/credit card scams, grandparent scams, DeepFakes, and physical tracking for kidnapping.
*   **DeepFakes** are a rising threat, using AI to impersonate individuals for malicious purposes.
*   **Over-sharing** on social media makes you a prime target for both digital and physical attacks.
*   **Protection** relies on strict rules: Never share credentials, think before posting, lock down your privacy settings, and always research unknown contacts.

### Frequently Asked Questions
**1. Are there other ways to reduce these scammers?**
**A:** Yes, there are plenty of ways. One way is to report them to the authorities. Additionally, there are content creators (like Jim Browning on YouTube) who dedicate themselves to hunting these scammers down and exposing their operations. You can watch videos on how they deal with these scammers to learn more about their tactics.

**2. Why is Social Engineering considered more dangerous than traditional hacking?**
**A:** Because it bypasses technical security controls entirely. It is much easier to trick a person into giving you their password than it is to crack a complex encryption algorithm. It relies on human error, which is difficult to patch with software.

**3. What should I do if I realize I've been social engineered?**
**A:** Immediately change your passwords for the compromised account and any other accounts that use the same password. Enable Two-Factor Authentication (2FA). Contact your bank or service provider immediately if financial information was shared. Report the incident to the authorities.

### Solo Project Update: Drafting Social Engineering Policies
**Project Overview:** 
For Module 20, you will expand your ongoing project by drafting a **Social Engineering Protection Policy** for your chosen organization. 

> *Note: This is a solo activity. You are acting as the sole security consultant.*

**Project Guidelines:**
*   **Format:** Individual work.
*   **Objective:** Create a security policy that addresses the risks of social engineering.
*   **Submission:** Add this to your ongoing project documentation.

**Step-by-Step Deliverable:**
Draft a 1-page policy document outlining the rules employees must follow to prevent social engineering attacks. Include specific policies based on the 4 protection rules discussed in this module.

**Draft your policy below:**

```markdown
# Social Engineering Protection Policy for [Project Name]

## 1. Credential Security
*   Employees must NEVER share their passwords, PINs, or login credentials with anyone, including IT support or management.
*   Passwords must be unique and meet the organization's complexity requirements.

## 2. Social Media and Public Information
*   Employees are discouraged from posting detailed personal information (home address, daily routines, children's school details) on public social media profiles.
*   Privacy settings must be set to the highest level.

## 3. Verification Protocol
*   Before clicking on any link or downloading an attachment from an unknown sender, employees must verify the sender's identity using a separate communication channel (e.g., calling the official number).
*   Any unsolicited request for credentials should be reported immediately to the IT Security team.

## 4. Incident Reporting
*   If an employee suspects they have been a victim of social engineering, they must report it to the IT Security team immediately so passwords can be changed and accounts locked.
```

*Answer the following questions with the best of your abilities.*

**1. As a student, how would you defend yourself from social engineer scammers/attackers?**
*Guidance:* I would defend myself by never sharing my passwords or login credentials. I would carefully check the URL of websites to avoid phishing sites. I would use Two-Factor Authentication (2FA) on all my accounts. I would also limit what I post on social media and avoid clicking on links from unknown senders. Finally, I would always verify the identity of anyone asking for information before responding.

**2. As a future parent, how would you protect your children against social engineer scammers/attackers?**
*Guidance:* As a parent, I would protect my children by monitoring their social media usage and teaching them about online safety, specifically about "stranger danger" online. I would configure strict privacy settings on their accounts so their location and school are not public. I would also teach them never to accept friend requests from people they don't know in real life and to immediately tell me if anyone online asks for personal information or money.

**3. Multiple Choice: Which of the following is an example of social engineering?**
A. A hacker exploiting a buffer overflow vulnerability in a web server.
B. A scammer calling an employee pretending to be from IT support to ask for their password.
C. Installing antivirus software on a computer.
D. Writing a secure SQL query.
**Correct Answer: B** (Social engineering relies on human interaction and manipulation).

**4. True or False: It is safe to share your PIN with your bank if they call you on the phone.**
**Correct Answer: False** (Banks never ask for your PIN over the phone. Anyone asking for this information is a scammer).

**5. Multiple Choice: What is the best way to protect yourself from a DeepFake impersonation attack?**
A. Trust every video you see on the internet.
B. Verify the identity of the person through a different, trusted communication channel (like a phone call to a known number).
C. Immediately send money if they ask.
D. Share your personal information to prove you are real.
**Correct Answer: B** (Verification is key to defeating impersonation attacks).
