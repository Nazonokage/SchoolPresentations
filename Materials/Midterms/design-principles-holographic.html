Design Fundamentals — a Field Guide

The Anatomy of Good Design

Thirteen principles that explain why some pages feel effortless to use — and why others feel off, even when you can't say exactly why. Plain-English, no jargon.

// THE FULL SET — 13 PRINCIPLES

01 · Where the eye goes first

Visual Hierarchy

Guide the eye to what matters most, in order — like a newspaper front page: biggest headline first, small print last.

Size

Bigger reads as more important. Headlines dwarf body text on purpose.

Color & contrast

Bold or bright elements grab attention first, before anything else.

Position

In left-to-right cultures, top-left is usually seen first.

Spacing

Elements set apart from the rest feel more significant.

02 · Distributing visual weight

Balance

A page shouldn't feel lopsided. There are two honest ways to balance it.

Symmetrical

A mirrored layout — the same weight on each side of a centerline. Feels formal, stable, trustworthy.

Asymmetrical

Different elements, similar visual "weight," balance each other. Feels modern and dynamic.

03 · 04 · Standing out, lining up

Contrast & Alignment

Two rules that make a page easy to scan and feel intentional, not accidental.

Contrast

The difference between elements — color, size, shape, weight — that creates emphasis. It's why a button doesn't get lost in a wall of text: it's crucial for readability and for drawing the eye to buttons, links, and headlines.

Alignment

Every element should line up with something else. Nothing sits randomly. Good alignment creates invisible lines that make a page feel clean and organized.

05 · 06 · Grouping & familiarity

Proximity & Repetition

These two rules quietly teach the reader how the page works, without a single label.

Proximity

Related items sit close together; unrelated items get space between them. This tells people what belongs together, no borders or labels needed.

Repetition

Reuse colors, fonts, button styles, and spacing throughout. Once someone learns how one button works, every button should work the same way.

07 · The room you leave

White Space

Beginners fear empty space and rush to fill it. Don't. Generous white space improves readability and focus — and it's the fastest way to make a design feel premium.

This slide has almost nothing on it. That's the point.

08 · The voice of the page

Typography

Type carries tone before anyone reads a single word.

The rules

• No more than 2–3 fonts per site
• A clear scale: H1 > H2 > H3 > body
• Line-height & letter-spacing affect readability
• Serif = traditional/editorial · Sans-serif = modern/clean

09 · Picking a palette

Color Theory

Pick a primary color, a secondary/accent, and neutrals — then keep them in proportion.

Primary + accent

One dominant color carries the brand; one accent highlights action.

60-30-10 rule

60% dominant, 30% secondary, 10% accent. It keeps a palette from feeling chaotic.

Accessible contrast

Text must stand clearly apart from its background — for everyone, not just some.

10 · The invisible skeleton

Grid & Layout

Sites are built on invisible grid systems — commonly 12 columns — to keep spacing and alignment consistent everywhere.

Think of it as graph paper

Every element snaps to a column, so nothing feels randomly placed — even across very different pages.

12 · Making it work, not just look good

Usability / UX Basics

People shouldn't have to "learn" your site. It should just make sense.

Clear navigation

People always know where they are and how to get back.

Obvious CTAs

Buttons look clickable, and the same action always looks the same.

Fast load times

Every second of delay costs attention — and trust.

Predictable

Interactions behave the way people already expect them to.

13 · Designing for everyone

Accessibility

Not an extra feature — a baseline. If it only works for some users, it isn't finished.

Color contrast

Text needs to stand out clearly from its background.

Readable size

Font sizes that don't force anyone to squint or zoom.

Alt text

Every image gets a text description for screen readers.

Keyboard access

Every element reachable and usable without a mouse.
