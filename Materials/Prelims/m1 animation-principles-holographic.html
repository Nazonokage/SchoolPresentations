Module 02 · Graphic Visual Computing

The 12 Principles of Animation

The rules Disney animators Frank Thomas and Ollie Johnston wrote down in the 1980s — and every game, UI, and motion designer still leans on today.

// THE FULL SET — 12 PRINCIPLES

01 · Weight and flexibility

Squash & Stretch

The single most important principle. It gives an object the illusion of weight, mass, and flexibility as it moves.

Bouncing ball

Squashes flat on impact, stretches long mid-air — the classic first exercise every animator learns.

The one rule

Volume stays constant. If a shape stretches taller, it must get thinner — otherwise it just looks like it's changing size, not deforming.

02 · Preparing the viewer

Anticipation

A smaller, opposite movement before the main action — it winds up the audience's expectations so the real action reads clearly.

Before a jump

A character crouches down first, then leaps up.

Before a punch

The fist pulls back before it swings forward.

Without it

Action feels sudden, robotic, or like the viewer missed something.

03 · One clear idea at a time

Staging

Present an idea so clearly the audience can't miss it — through pose, camera angle, lighting, and composition.

Do this

One clear focal point. Silhouette reads instantly, even in shadow. Only the essential action is shown at once.

Not this

Too much happening at once, competing actions, or a pose that reads as ambiguous from the camera's angle.

04 · Two ways to work

Straight Ahead & Pose to Pose

Two different workflows for building the same motion — most animators end up mixing both.

Straight ahead

Draw frame by frame, in order, from start to finish. Feels spontaneous and fluid — great for fire, water, or wild action — but harder to keep on-model.

Pose to pose

Plan key poses first, then fill in the frames between. Easier to control timing and composition — the standard approach for dialogue and character acting.

05 · Nothing stops all at once

Follow Through & Overlapping Action

Different parts of a body move at different rates and stop at different times — nothing is perfectly rigid.

Follow through

Loose parts — hair, ears, a coat — keep moving briefly after the main body has stopped.

Overlapping action

Different body parts move on their own slightly offset timing, instead of all moving in perfect unison.

06 · Easing the motion

Slow In & Slow Out

Real objects accelerate and decelerate — they don't snap instantly from still to full speed.

More frames near the poses, fewer in between

Bunch up drawings close to the start and end of a movement, and space them further apart in the middle. This is exactly what CSS easing curves (ease-in, ease-out) do on a screen.

07 · The path of natural motion

Arcs

Almost all living motion follows a curved path — arms, eyes, heads, and thrown objects all travel in arcs, not straight lines.

08 · Supporting the main idea

Secondary Action

A smaller motion that adds richness without competing with — or distracting from — the main action.

Example

A character walking (main action) while their tail swishes (secondary action).

The rule

It must support the main action, never pull focus away from it.

In UI terms

A subtle icon wiggle alongside a primary button press.

09 · How fast, how slow

Timing

The number of frames used for an action sets its speed — and speed itself carries emotional meaning.

Fast timing

Reads as light, urgent, energetic, or comedic — a slap, a jump scare, a quick reaction.

Slow timing

Reads as heavy, deliberate, sad, or powerful — a slow nod, a heavy door swinging shut.

10 · Pushing past realism

Exaggeration

Push a pose, expression, or action further than real life — realism alone often reads as flat or lifeless on screen.

Not about being unrealistic for its own sake

It's about amplifying the essence of an action so it reads clearly and with more energy — a wide-eyed shock, an extra-deep crouch before a jump, an overly dramatic sigh.

11 · Existing in 3D space

Solid Drawing

A character needs to feel like it has real volume, weight, and balance — as if it exists in three dimensions, not just flat on a page.

Volume

Shapes read as solid forms, not flat silhouettes.

Weight

Poses look like they're affected by gravity and mass.

Balance

A pose looks stable and physically believable.

12 · Pleasant to watch

Appeal

A character should be interesting and pleasing to look at — this applies to heroes and villains alike.

Appeal ≠ cute

A terrifying villain can still have strong appeal through a clear, well-designed silhouette, expressive features, and confident design choices. What kills appeal is a design that's cluttered, confusing, or hard to read at a glance.

Putting it together
