Zooming out to the methodology

In a nutshell

The design process is entirely structured around the specific, real-world tasks a user needs to accomplish. Instead of adding cool features, ask: what is the user trying to get done, and how can we make that as smooth as possible?

Step 1

Figure out who will use the system to do what

Task and user analysis. A successful system merges smoothly into the user’s existing workflow.

Natural order

Request information in the order the user naturally receives it.

Easy correction

Make it easy to correct frequent errors.

Background knowledge

Mac users expect standard cut, copy and paste.

Less quantifiable factors

Confidence and willingness to learn new systems dictate how much feedback to provide.

Steps 2 and 3

Real tasks, and reuse good design

2. Choose representative tasks

Pick 3 to 5 real tasks, not abstract concepts. A mix of simple and complex, with the actual materials collected to cover the system’s functionality.

  • Spreadsheet: “Produce a salary budget for next year.”
  • Comms program: “Log into the office via modem.”

3. “Plagiarize”

Do not reinvent the wheel. Reuse interfaces that already work for users.

  • Familiar with spreadsheets? Make data entry look like one.
  • Building a spell checker? Copy Word’s button placement and menu naming.

Quicker to build, easier to learn.

Steps 4 and 5

Paper first, then analyze

4. Rough out the design

Get it out of your head and onto paper. Do not program yet: coding early commits you to decisions you have not thought through.

For any proposed feature ask: which representative task supports this? If none, discard it.

5. Think about it

Like aviation engineers simulating before building a jet, analyze the design first.

GOMS analysis

Goals, Operators, Methods, Selection rules. Count keystrokes and mental operations to estimate task times and find slow steps.

Cognitive walkthrough

Analyze the interface to spot where users might make mistakes.

Step 6

Create a mock-up or prototype

Paper sketches

A user steps through a task. Cheap, and reveals hidden misunderstandings.

Digital prototypes

Move to digital prototyping tools.

Wizard of Oz technique

If the backend is not built, a human behind the screen responds manually to simulate a working system.

Steps 7 and 8

Test, then iterate

7. Test with users

  • Match the target audience’s background.
  • Ask them to perform the representative tasks.
  • Ask them to think aloud.
  • Document time taken, errors made and confusion.

8. Iterate

Testing is not to prove the interface works. It is to find what needs improving.

  • Balance fix cost against severity.
  • Revise and test again.
  • Fixing a menu for one task might break another.
  • Stop when usability objectives are met.
Steps 9 to 11

Build, track and change

9. Build the design

Now you code. Build for change: move from the prototyping system to a proper environment, avoid hardcoded parameters, and write modular code.

10. Track the design

Rotate designers onto the customer hotline and hold user group meetings. It reveals real needs and new market opportunities.

11. Change the design

Software that is not updated becomes obsolete within a few years. Tasks change, users gain skills and new hardware emerges.

Fred Stroller

Fred is a slow typist buying a red JPG stroller. A task-centered design minimizes his typing, with large clickable category buttons instead of text search, and shows the total cost clearly before he triggers the invoice.

Recap

Four things to take with you

Task-centered design focuses on real tasks, not abstract features.
Plan on paper and use cheap prototypes before code.
Reusing existing patterns makes an app easier to learn.
Testing and iterating are the most critical steps.

Put Fred’s tasks in order

  • Fred enters the store
  • Fred looks at the computer
  • Fred moves to the phone field
  • Fred enters his name in the computer
  • Fred fills in the phone, postal code, province, and city
  • Fred enters his delivery address
  • Fred looks for a catalog
  • Fred enters his credit card info
  • Fred finds the red JPG stroller in the catalog
