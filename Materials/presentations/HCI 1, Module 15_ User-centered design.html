User-centered design

An iterative process that puts users at the center of product design and development.

Press ? for shortcuts.

Lesson preview

The most important part of any system

In a nutshell

Understand who your users are and what they need, design a solution for them, then constantly test and tweak it based on real-world feedback.

Instead of designing for what looks cool or is easiest to code, UCD takes users’ requirements, objectives and feedback into account in every phase.

Four phases

An iterative loop, rarely a straight line

1. Understand context of use

Who the users are, what they are trying to do, and under what conditions.

2. Specify user requirements

Exactly what users need the system to do.

3. Design solutions

Sketches, wireframes and prototypes based on the requirements.

4. Evaluate against requirements

Test with real users to see whether it solves their problem.

The team cycles until evaluation is satisfactory. It mixes ethnographers, psychologists, engineers, domain experts and the users themselves, using surveys, interviews and brainstorming.

UCD vs HCD

A subset of human-centered design

Simply put

All users are humans, but not all humans will be your users.

Why it matters

UCD needs deeper analysis of a specific audience: age, gender, education and particular habits, to solve specific problems.

Five major principles

What UCD rests on

1. A clear understanding of user and task requirements.
2. Incorporating user feedback to define requirements and design.
3. Early and active involvement of the user to evaluate the design.
4. Integrating UCD with other development activities.
5. An iterative design process.

Why it matters

Changing a design late typically costs ten times more than catching the issue at the requirements stage. Analysis and feedback save time and money.

Elements and drawbacks

What good looks like, and where UCD stumbles

Essential elements

Visibility

See immediately what you can do and what the product is about.

Accessibility

Find information quickly in several ways: search, menu, call-to-action buttons.

Legibility

Easy to read: good contrast, proper size.

Language

Short, clear sentences. The easier the words, the better.

Drawbacks

Passive involvement

Users might not give active, useful feedback.

Inappropriate perception

Users might expect the wrong thing from the new interface.

Wrong questions

Designers might ask questions that produce bad data.

Seven ways, part 1

Concentrate, simplify, respond, organize

1. Visibility

Vital elements like navigation must be conspicuous. Cisco’s nav bar is ever-present and grabbing, giving confidence and order.

2. Minimal memory load

Do not make users recall an icon from the last page. MSN.com keeps its nav bar and layout identical on every page.

3. Instant feedback

Something on the site must change after an action. On Microsoft’s site, “products” instantly drops down a menu.

4. Accessibility

Good organization, search or a sitemap, in digestible sections. Apple splits its Mac page into buying, customization and specs.

Seven ways, part 2

Orient, please, and tie it together

5. Site orientation

Navigational clues: sitemaps, descriptive links and highly visible breadcrumbs. PayPal’s sitemap gives absolute orientation.

6. Make it pleasant

Simple to use and look at. Flickr shows endless matching photos right after a search: positive reinforcement.

7. Visual design

Aesthetics that communicate tone and information, with sparing color. Sony’s homepage is simple yet eye-catching.

Real-life scenarios

A car dashboard and the modern toolkit

Visibility

See the speedometer and fuel gauge without looking away from the road.

Memory load

The pedals are always in the same place.

Instant feedback

Press the gas and the car accelerates, with no 5-second pause.

Site orientation

The GPS shows where you are relative to your destination.

Modern application

UCD is the foundation of UX design. Companies run heatmaps to see where users click and A/B tests to compare two designs. Agile development leans on the same iterative loop.

Recap

Four things to take with you

UCD is an iterative process with the user at the center.
It needs a multi-disciplinary team and continuous feedback.
A user-centered site has visibility, low memory load, instant feedback, accessibility, orientation, pleasantness and strong visual design.
Fixing flaws early saves 10x the cost.
