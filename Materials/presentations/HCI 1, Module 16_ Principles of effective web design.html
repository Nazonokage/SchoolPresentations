Usability and utility decide success

In a nutshell

Success is determined by usability and utility, not just looks. The visitor is the only person who clicks the mouse, so a feature they cannot figure out might as well not exist.

How users think

Six behaviors, popularized by Steve Krug

Quality and credibility

Great content earns forgiveness for minor design flaws.

They scan

Users hunt for fixed points or anchors. They rarely read linearly.

They are impatient

Instant gratification or they click Back.

They satisfice

They choose the first reasonable option, not the quickest.

They follow intuition

They muddle through instead of reading instructions.

They want control

They rely on Back. Never open links in new windows unexpectedly.

Principles 1 and 2

Do not make users think, or waste patience

1. Don’t make users think

Krug’s first law: pages should be obvious and self-explanatory. Remove the “question marks.”

Modern: clear microcopy on buttons and forms.

Vague versus clear.

2. Don’t squander patience

First-time visitors will play with a service but not fill out a long registration form to test it. Let them explore before demanding private data.

Modern: Guest Checkout. Forced accounts cause massive cart abandonment.

Principles 3 and 4

Focus attention, expose features

3. Focus users’ attention

Images catch the eye more than text, and bold more than plain. Guide focus to what matters. The “Humanized” site centers on the word FREE as an informative anchor.

4. Strive for feature exposure

Visually appealing “1-2-3 done” steps and large buttons with visual effects lead visitors through the content in a simple, friendly way.

Principles 5 and 6

Write for the web, keep it simple

5. Effective writing

The web is not print. Long blocks without images get skipped, and exaggerated marketing is ignored.

  • Talk business. Say “Sign up,” not “Start now!” or “Explore our services.”
  • Short phrases, multiple heading levels, bulleted lists.

6. Strive for simplicity (KIS)

“Keep It Simple” is the primary goal. To the visitor, the best design is a pure text layout without distracting ads or unnecessary blocks.

Principle 7

Don’t be afraid of white space

Less cognitive load

White (negative) space makes information easier to digest.

Simon’s Law

Hierarchical structures reduce complexity. Separate segments with whitespace rather than visible lines.

Principle 8

Communicate with a visible language

Aaron Marcus: three fundamental principles of visual communication on screen.

Organize

A clear, consistent conceptual structure: consistency and screen layout.

Economize

Do the most with the least: simplicity, clarity, distinctiveness, emphasis.

Communicate

Match presentation to the user’s capabilities.

Principle 9

Conventions are our friends

Conventional design does not make a boring website. It makes an intuitive one, and it reduces the learning curve.

Cart top right

Users expect the shopping cart icon in the top right.

Logo top left

And the logo in the top left.

Hamburger menu

Three horizontal lines. A modern convention users already understand.

Principle 10

Test early, test often (TETO)

Steve Krug

Testing one user is 100 percent better than testing none. One user early beats 50 near the end.

Boehm’s First Law

Errors are most frequent in requirements and design, and cost more the later they are removed.

Weinberg’s Law

A developer is unsuited to test their own code. Designers suffer the curse of knowledge.

Real-life scenarios

A soft opening and a strange car

Restaurant soft opening (TETO)

Do not wait for opening night with 500 reservations. Test with a few friends and family first: is the menu clear, the kitchen too slow, the dining room comfortable?

Power of conventions

Imagine the steering wheel in a new place each time, the gas pedal on the left and the brake on the ceiling. You would crash. Because search bars and carts stay put, we do not relearn the internet every day.

Recap

Five things to take with you

Users scan, they do not read.
Do not make users think: use conventions and clear layouts.
Respect patience: avoid forced logins.
White space and hierarchy reduce cognitive load.
TETO: designers cannot objectively test their own work.
