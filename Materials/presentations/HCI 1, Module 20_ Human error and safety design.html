Human error and safety design

Assume mistakes will happen. Build interfaces that prevent, catch or easily recover from them.

Lesson preview

We all make mistakes

A typo, a mis-click or the wrong information in a form can mean frustration, or even accidental financial loss.

In a nutshell

You cannot trust users to always do the right thing. Your job is to assume human error will happen and build systems that prevent, catch, or easily recover from it.

Nine ways, part 1

Instructions, warnings and spacing

1. Clear instructions

People skip them, assuming they know. Put concise instructions on anything more complex than a contact form, with tooltips or inline help.

2. Clear warnings

Warn clearly before destructive actions. Beware alert fatigue: warn about everything and users ignore everything.

3. White space

Fat-finger taps are common. Give opposing buttons like Delete and Save ample negative space.

Nine ways, part 2

Confirmations and validation

4. Confirmation dialogs

“Are you sure?” for destructive actions, used sparingly. For repetitive tasks they cause dialog blindness: users click Yes without reading.

5. Form validation

Do not wait for Submit. Validate as users type or leave the field, with a green border for valid and red with a message for invalid.

Bad versus good message

Error 404: Invalid input.

Please enter a valid email address (e.g., name@example.com).

Try inline validation

Enter an email like name@example.com.

Border, icon and text all change, never color alone.

Nine ways, part 3

Labels and color

6. Clear labels

Vague labels and jargon cause errors and abandonment. Placeholders vanish when typing begins, so always use a persistent label.

Placeholder only

7. Use both color and contrast

Color-blind users may not tell red from green. Pair color with contrast, text or icons.

Color alone: a red border and nothing else.

Better: a red border plus “!” and “Invalid email format.”

Nine ways, part 4

Change carefully, and undo

8. Make changes carefully

People run on autopilot. Move a menu item and muscle memory clicks the wrong thing. Jakob’s Law: users spend most time on other sites. When redesigning, use feature highlights or onboarding tours.

9. Undo where possible

The ultimate safety net. The modern standard is the snackbar or toast, like Gmail’s “moved to Trash. Undo.” Recovery without interrupting flow.

Real-life scenarios

Poka-yoke and the danger zone

Poka-yoke

Japanese for mistake-proofing. A microwave cannot start with its door open. Disabling Submit until required fields are filled is poka-yoke.

The risk

Edit Profile and Delete User side by side. One mis-click permanently deletes the account.

Danger zone

Recap

Five things to take with you

Assume users will make mistakes and design to prevent them.
White space and clear labels prevent mis-clicks and confusion.
Warnings and confirmations for destructive actions, but not too many.
Form validation guides users in real time.
Always offer Undo, like a snackbar, as the final safety net.
Activity: true or false

TRUE or FALSE?
