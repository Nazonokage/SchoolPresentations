Lesson preview

A new era of HCI

As technology pervades every part of life, especially AI and machine learning, our basic idea of an “interface” is changing.

In a nutshell

The future is more natural (eyes, voice, body) and more immersive (VR and AR). With great technological power comes great ethical responsibility for privacy, bias and accessibility.

Future themes to present-day HCI.

Theme 1

Eye-tracking

Look at a character and the system responds instantly.

How it works

Cameras measure the gaze point and how your pupils react.

The potential

Eyes reveal intention and subconscious cues. Used in accessibility, so paralyzed users can type with their eyes, and in UX research.

Modern context

Apple Vision Pro navigates by eye. Foveated rendering draws high resolution only where you look, saving computing power.

Theme 2

Voice-guided user interfaces

Beyond timers

As screens shrink or disappear, touch gets harder. Voice promises deeper, ubiquitous interaction.

Modern context

With large language models like ChatGPT, assistants like Siri and Alexa are becoming conversational, moving from commands to natural dialogue.

80%+

The predicted adoption rate of speech recognition in the near future, per the module.

Theme 3

Virtual and augmented reality

VR

A fully immersive, computer-generated environment, like Meta Quest or PlayStation VR.

AR

Digital elements overlaid on the real world, like Google Glass or Microsoft HoloLens.

The shift

UI/UX work will blur with 3D animation. Learn 3D design to stay relevant. Apple’s spatial computing signals the next platform after the smartphone.

Theme 4

Wearables and bio-integration

Today

Devices like the Apple Watch brought unique interactions: the digital crown and biometric sensors. Wearables will get cheaper, more functional and less phone-dependent.

The future

Tech injected into our bodies could monitor vital signs constantly with no visible hardware.

Modern context

Smart rings like Oura, continuous glucose monitors, and the growing Quantified Self movement.

Ethics

Four implications designers will face

Privacy and data collection

If a wearable monitors your heart 24/7 or a headset tracks your eyes, who owns and protects that data?

Algorithmic bias

AI is only as good as its training data. Facial recognition trained mostly on white faces fails people with darker skin.

The digital divide

Will advanced, expensive tech split those who can afford headsets and bio-tech from those who cannot?

Invisible interfaces

When tech is embedded and always listening, users forget they are tracked and lose control of their spaces.

Real-life scenarios

Minority Report and eye-controlled access

The Minority Report analogy

Swiping files in mid-air with gestures and eye tracking. We are not there yet, but spatial computing aims to make technology feel like an extension of your own body.

Accessibility via eye-tracking

For people with severe motor impairments like ALS or paralysis, eye-tracking lets them use a computer, speak via text-to-speech and control a light using only their eyes. HCI improving human life.

Recap

Five things to take with you

Eye-tracking allows hands-free interaction and reveals subconscious intent.
Voice UI grows as screens shrink and AI becomes conversational.
VR and AR break interfaces out of flat screens into 3D space.
Wearables move toward constant biometric monitoring.
Ethics: protect privacy and keep AI unbiased.
