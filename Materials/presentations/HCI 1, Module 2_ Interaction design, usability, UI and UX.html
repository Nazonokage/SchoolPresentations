Main lesson

Interaction design and goal-driven design

Interaction design

Began with the move from static web pages to interactive experiences. Users could now click, tap and swipe. Good interaction design focuses on the experience of using a system, not just how it looks.

Goal-driven design

A methodology popularized by Alan Cooper. It solves the specific needs and desires of the end-user instead of focusing on what technology can do.

Goal-driven design

Five principles, one question

Design first; program second

Focus on how users interact and how things look before technical constraints.

Separate design from programming

Designers champion the end-user and trust developers with the technical side.

Designers own quality

Clients have objectives, but the designer’s first duty is the person on the other side of the screen.

Use personas

Tie each decision to a specific user profile.

Work in teams

Never solo. Pair with copywriters, project managers and information architects.

Ask

Who is this person? What do they want to accomplish?

Usability

A usable system answers: “Can someone easily use this?”

Easy to learn

New users quickly figure out how to accomplish tasks.

Easy to remember

Returning users do not have to relearn the system.

Effective to use

It helps users achieve their goals accurately.

Efficient to use

Tasks finish in a timely manner, with no unnecessary steps.

Safe to use

It protects users from irreversible mistakes.

Enjoyable to use

The experience is satisfying and frustration-free.

Five dimensions

The language of interaction design

Introduced by Gillian Crampton Smith, later expanded by Kevin Silver.

Words: simple, clear, easy to understand.
Visuals: graphics, images and icons, in moderation.
Physical: the hardware, like a mouse, keyboard or phone.
Time: how long users spend with the first three, including sound, animation and progress.
Behavior: the emotions and reactions users have.
Cognitive psychology

How the mind works, applied to design

The study of attention, language, memory, perception, problem-solving and creativity.

Mental models

Images in a user’s mind that shape what they expect. Understand them and the system feels intuitive.

Interface metaphors

Known actions lead users to new ones. A trash can icon means delete.

Affordances

Things that look designed to do something, like a button that looks pushable.

UI vs UX

Often swapped, but different

User interface (UI)User experience (UX)
FocusVisual and interactive elements: looks and style.The entire process of acquiring and integrating a product.
ScopeA subset of UX. Focuses on the access points.The umbrella: branding, design, usability and function.
GoalInterfaces that are easy to use and pleasurable.Meaningful, seamless, fluid experiences.

Think of a car: UI is the dashboard, UX is the whole driving experience.

Interface formats

Three ways to touch a system

Graphical (GUI)

Visual representations on digital panels, like a computer desktop.

Voice-controlled (VUI)

Interaction through speech, like Siri or Alexa.

Gesture-based

Bodily motions in 3D spaces, like VR games.

UX framework: start with Why

Why is the user’s motivation. What is the product functionality. How is designing that functionality to be accessible.

Real-life scenarios

Where the ideas bite

The photocopier fail

Copies: 15

Which button starts the job? Try the one labeled C.

Why it failed

Poor words and a confusing visual broke the user’s mental model.

The mouse

UX is how it fits your hand and its weight. UI is where the buttons and scroll wheel sit.

A food app

Why: hungry, wants food fast. What: browse and order. How: filter by cuisine, checkout in one tap.

Recap

Five things to take with you

Goal-driven design serves user needs, not technological capability.
Usability means easy to learn, remember, effective, efficient, safe and enjoyable.
Interaction design has five dimensions: words, visuals, space, time and behavior.
Cognitive psychology gives us mental models, metaphors and affordances.
UI is the visual touchpoints. UX is the holistic experience.
