Part 1: elements of design

Color, line and value

Color

Sets the mood. It is the sensation when light waves reflect to the optic nerve. Blue often conveys trust and calm, red urgency or energy.

Line

How two points in space are connected. Lines direct the eye, and curved or patterned lines can create texture.

Value

The lightness or darkness of a color. It creates the illusion of mass, volume and depth in 2D.

Part 1: elements of design

Space

Negative space

Also white space: the space between or around the focal point.

Positive space

The space your subject matter takes up.

Rule of thumb

A layout that is too crowded can overwhelm the viewer’s eye.

Part 1: elements of design

Shape

A shape is a two-dimensional area surrounded by an outline.

Organic

Occur naturally, like a leaf or a cloud.

Geometric

Angular and mathematically consistent, like squares and triangles.

Abstract

Represent nature but are not perfectly representative, like a stylized human icon.

Part 1: elements of design

Form and texture

Form is the illusion of 3D on a flat surface, made with light, shadow, contours and negative space.

Form

How a shape occupies space. Shape is 2D. Form adds depth.

Tactile texture

The physical sense of touch: rough, smooth, ribbed.

Visual texture

The imagined feel of an illustrated texture. It adds visual interest and a heightened sensory experience.

Part 2

Sketches come first, wireframes second

Sketch

Rapid freehand drawing with no intent of becoming a finished product. Separates design from making.

  • Quick, timely, inexpensive
  • Disposable and plentiful
  • Fluid and clear
  • Minimally detailed and ambiguous

Wireframe

A basic visual guide suggesting structure and the relationships between pages. A thinking device that outlines the flow.

  • Viable: narrowed to the best options
  • Confirmatory: concepts needing validation
  • Appropriately refined: clear structure with simple symbols
Part 3

Seven steps to a wireframe

1. Identify the goal

What should visitors do? Align your team first.

2. Understand the user flow

Map the journey from the homepage. Outline it in text first.

3. Determine the size

Use pixels, not inches.

4. Begin the design

Dotted or grid paper by hand. Pick a low, medium or high fidelity tool.

5. Determine conversion points

Which buttons, links or images lead to the end goal?

6. Remove redundant steps

Fewer clicks. Wireframing is iterative.

7. Get feedback

From designers, developers and staff before final layouts.

Real-life scenarios

Napkin versus blueprint

The architect analogy

The napkin is a sketch: messy and fast. The blueprint is a wireframe: precise, and the guide for builders. You would not hand a napkin to a construction crew.

E-commerce checkout

Goal: the user completes a purchase.

Wireframe: low-fidelity cart, shipping and payment pages.

Conversion point: a big, clear “Proceed to Payment” button.

Redundant step removed: a “Guest Checkout” button instead of forced account creation.
