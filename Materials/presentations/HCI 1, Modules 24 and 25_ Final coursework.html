The capstone

You will act as both UX/UI designer and front-end developer, applying HCI concepts so your product is usable and accessible, not just pretty.

In a nutshell

The real-world development lifecycle: pitch an idea (Proposal), draw it (Wireframes and Mockups), write the code (Coding), then get feedback (Evaluation).

Identify

The steps from concept to code.

Understand

Why planning, prototyping and evaluating matter.

Create

A proposal, design plan, working prototype and final coded site.

Roadmap

Six phases

1. Proposal (ideation)
2. Sketches, wireframes, storyboard, mockups
3. Target presentation (pitching)
4. Coding and content
5. Prototype (mid-point check)
6. Evaluation (feedback and iteration)

Move through the phases systematically. Phases 1 to 3 are planning, 4 is building, 5 and 6 are checking.

Phase 1

Proposal: every great website starts with a clear idea

Requirements

  • Title or name of the page.
  • List of possible content: text and images.
  • List of possible features: search bar, contact form, image slider.
  • HCI application: how user-centered design, usability or accessibility will apply.

Modern addendum

Add a user persona, a fictional ideal user, and a statement of purpose, like “This site will help college students find affordable textbooks.”

Phase 2

Design: sketches, wireframes, storyboard and mockups

Follow user-centered or task-centered design principles.

Sketch

Rough, hand-drawn content ideas.

Wireframes

Low-fidelity structural outlines, like blueprints.

Storyboard

A sequential visual narrative of how a user navigates the features.

Mockups

High-fidelity visuals with colors, real text and images.

Modern addendum

Hand-draw the sketch. Use Figma, Adobe XD or Balsamiq for wireframes and mockups.

Phase 3

Target presentation: pitch for early feedback

What to do

Present your plan and design to your instructor and classmates.

Requirements

A complete list of features and content, and a complete mock design of the website.

The goal

Early feedback. Revisions are much easier before a single line of code.

Phase 4

Coding and content: follow your designs

index.html
<!DOCTYPE html>
<html lang="en">
<head>
  <meta charset="utf-8">
  <meta name="viewport" content="width=device-width, initial-scale=1.0">
  <title>Affordable Textbooks</title>
</head>
<body>
  <h1>Find Affordable Textbooks</h1>
  <h2>Search by course</h2>
  <img src="books.jpg" alt="A stack of used textbooks on a desk">
</body>
</html>

Requirements

The HTML file and related files: images, audio, CSS.

HCI focus

Semantic heading structure, alt text on images, and the viewport meta tag for responsiveness.

Modern addendum

Real sites also need CSS and JavaScript. Use VS Code, and Git for version control so you never lose progress.

Phases 5 and 6

Prototype check, then evaluation

5. Prototype (mid-point check)

Present your progress to confirm you are on track and surface roadblocks.

  • A working prototype of the proposed project.
  • More than 50 percent complete.

6. Evaluation

Other groups evaluate your project, and the teacher gives feedback. Use it to improve.

  • A progress rating from another group (peer review).
  • A progress rating from your teacher.

Modern addendum: usability testing

Give a user a task, like “Find the contact page,” and watch without helping. Their struggles are your most valuable data.

Working as a team

Roles, effort and scope

Divide the work in Phase 1

A Lead Designer for Phase 2, a Lead Coder for Phase 4, and a QA or project manager. Track it in Google Docs or Trello.

The hardest phase

Many find Phase 4 most challenging: drawing a button is easy, linking it to another page is harder. Start coding early and test in your browser often.

No backend needed

You only need the HTML file and related media files. You are building the interface, unless your instructor says otherwise.

Real-life scenarios

A house build and a 20-hour mistake

The construction analogy

Proposal: “a 3-bedroom house with a big kitchen.” Design: blueprints and a 3D rendering. Coding: pour the foundation, build and paint. Evaluation: the walkthrough finds a misplaced switch before handover.

Why Phase 3 is crucial

Spend 20 hours coding a complex menu, then learn in Phase 6 that users want search. 20 hours wasted. Pitch the menu in Phase 3 and you catch it after 1 hour of drawing in Figma.

Recap

Four things to take with you

Planning prevents poor performance. Phases 1 to 3 matter as much as coding.
Iteration is key. Present at Phases 3 and 5 to fix mistakes before submission.
Always refer back to your wireframes and mockups.
You are graded on applying usability and accessibility to your real code and design.
