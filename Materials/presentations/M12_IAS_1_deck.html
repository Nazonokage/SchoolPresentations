Authentication: proving you are who you say you are

Identity first, then access, then a report on every invalid attempt.

Preview and review

Authentication vs authorization

The memo analogy
The Dean announces no class today, by memo. We know the Dean is authorized to share that. But how do we know the memo really came from the Dean? We check the signature. That signature is the authentication: it proves the identity of the sender.

What is authentication?

Proving the right person is accessing

Definition
The process of proving that the person trying to access a document, system or resource is the correct, authorized person.
It can be done by
  • Providing a password to access an account
  • Providing a PIN or code
  • Using biometric data (fingerprint, facial recognition)

Pro tip: your smartphone is only authorized for you or people you trust. To let others in, you authenticate them with your password or biometric access.

The authentication process

Three key steps

1. Granting access rights
Give authorized users the authentication code or rights by creating accounts or issuing access codes.
2. Allowing authorized access
Validate the access rights given and allow access to what is authorized.
3. Reporting invalid entries
User: notified by email or notification (“Someone tried to log into your Facebook account”). Admin: also notified; many invalid attempts could be a DoS attack, and officers must stop it immediately.
Why it matters

Importance and better passwords

Importance
To prove that the one accessing something is truly the authorized user. Without it, anyone could impersonate a user, reach sensitive data or act without authorization.
Better password practices
Passwords often need a capital letter, a symbol, a number and a few letters. With these categories, computers take years to crack or guess it. It also helps if it cannot be found in the dictionary.
Two-factor authentication

One authentication is good; two is better

How it works
Log in with your password (factor 1). The system sends a one-time password (OTP) to your registered phone (factor 2). Enter it to gain access. Facebook does this when you log in from a different device.
If authentication fails
Losing an account you cannot recover is a real loss. Have recovery processes: a trusted family member or significant other, a secret email address, a physical notebook with your basic password, or security questions.
TypeRequiredSecurity level
Single-factorPassword onlyLow (vulnerable to phishing and guessing)
Two-factor (2FA)Password + OTP, hardware key or biometricsHigh (needs a second physical or biometric check)

Analogy and use case

VIP club and online banking

VIP club
Authorization: you are on the guest list. Authentication: the bouncer checks your ID. 2FA: the bouncer also asks for a secret password texted to your phone when you RSVP’d.
Online banking
1. Username and password. 2. From a new location, the bank asks for an OTP sent to your phone (2FA). 3. After 5 wrong passwords the bank locks the account and emails an alert (admin and user reporting), preventing a brute-force attack.
Course project

Authentication recommendations

Solo steps
  • Review the current setup: passwords? signed memos? email system?
  • Draft a short section for the “Security Policies” chapter
  • Password policy (for example, minimum 12 characters, symbols, rotate every 90 days)
  • 2FA: where to require it (email, financial systems)
  • Memo authentication: every memo signed by its writer
  • Recovery plan for forgotten passwords
Example: small clinic
Passwords: minimum 10 characters with a number and a symbol; change every 60 days. 2FA: mandatory for staff email and the Electronic Health Records system. Physical: patient files signed out with an authenticated staff ID badge. Recovery: contact the IT admin and answer two pre-set security questions.
Questions answered

Frequently asked

Why 2FA?
Even if a hacker steals your password, they cannot get in without the second factor.
A strong password?
Upper and lower case, numbers and symbols. Avoid dictionary words, personal information like birthdays, and reuse. A password manager can help.
Authentication vs authorization?
Authentication verifies who you are; authorization determines what you can do.
Why report invalid attempts?
Repeated failures can signal a DoS or brute-force attempt. Reporting lets teams stop it before the system is compromised.
Key quotes

Definitions worth remembering

Authentication
The process of proving identity.
Authorization
The process of granting rights and permissions.
Reporting
A robust process reports invalid attempts to both users and admins.
Recovery
Always have an account recovery plan in place.
Recap

Key takeaways

  • Authentication proves identity; authorization grants rights.
  • The process: grant access, allow valid access, report invalid attempts.
  • Strong passwords and 2FA drastically improve security.
  • Always have an account recovery plan.
