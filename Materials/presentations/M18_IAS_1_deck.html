Step 3

Implementing security controls

TypeHow it is implementedRequired documentation
PhysicalHiring a security guard, installing locks, building fencesRequest forms, purchase orders, installation logs
AdministrativeUpdating security policies, conducting trainingUpdated policy manuals, employee training records, memos
TechnicalBuying software, configuring firewalls, patching systemsIT job tickets, software licenses, configuration logs

In every scenario, documentation is the critical component. As a security officer you recommend; the organization enacts. State the risk, the recommended control type and function, and the specific solution.

Writing recommendations

Two formats

Enumerated
Risk: physical theft of office equipment or assets.
Type and function: preventive physical and detective physical security.
Recommendation: hire a security guard to check and protect the premises, and equip rooms and hallways with CCTV to detect intruders.
Paragraph
On the risk of flooding, we recommend a preventive administrative security control: a policy that mandates moving all water-vulnerable assets to higher ground to prevent destruction from floods.
Step 4

Three assessment questions

1. Enough?
Is the recommendation enough, or do we need something more? Hypothesize what happens if the risk occurs; if the control was not enough to prevent, detect or correct it, adjust or increase it.
2. Cheaper?
Is there a cheaper option? Often executives’ most important question. Sometimes CCTV is more cost-effective and sufficient compared to a full-time guard.
3. Configured?
Is the risk completely correct, or does it need configuration? Answer by choosing one of the four risk treatment options.

The assessor is usually the organization’s security officer or an assigned employee. Your job is to create the framework for them.

Risk treatment options

Avoid, reduce, transfer, accept

OptionDescriptionGoalFrequency
AvoidEliminate the risk by avoiding the activity that causes it100% eliminationRare (only works for 1/3 of all risks)
ReduceMitigate damage through the correct security controlReduce likelihood or impactMost common
TransferShift the risk to a third party such as an insurer or parent organizationShare the burden of damageRare (needs a third party)
AcceptAcknowledge the risk and take no action because the damage is too smallMonitor onlyLeast recommended (worst option)

Analogy and use case

Home alarm and hospital breach

Home renovation
Step 3: hire an electrician, buy the alarm, document the installation. Step 4: (1) enough? add window sensors. (2) cheaper? a smart alarm instead of a $200/month service. (3) configuration? “pet-friendly” mode so the dog does not trigger it (reduce).
Hospital data breach
Technical / preventive: $50,000 encryption software. Enough? Also train staff (administrative). Cheaper? A cloud version at $10,000 a year. Treatment: reduce with cloud encryption and transfer the remaining risk to cyber insurance.

Recommendations and assessment framework

Part 1: Recommendations chapter
Write a formal recommendation for every risk in your RMF table. Draft at least 2, in enumerated or paragraph format: Risk addressed and Recommended control (type, function and specific action).
Part 2: Recommended Assessment
Tell the organization that every recommendation must be assessed. Draft how they should answer the 3 questions: enough? cheaper? which of avoid, reduce, transfer or accept?
Questions answered

Frequently asked

Who assesses controls?
Usually the manager or executive assigned to that department, who monitors the risk and is responsible for the assets.
Why is “accept” the worst option?
The organization does nothing, so if the risk happens it takes the full damage. Only for truly negligible damage.
Implement without documentation?
No. Undocumented controls cannot be tracked, audited or assessed.
Cheapest is not best?
Aim for best value: effective and cost-efficient. A cheaper option that leaves a critical vulnerability open is not a good recommendation.

Lines worth remembering

Recap

Key takeaways

  • Step 3 needs documentation: requests, policies, tickets.
  • Step 4 asks three questions about every recommendation.
  • Four risk treatment options: avoid, reduce, transfer, accept.
  • Reduce is most common; accept is least recommended.
Activity 1

Identification: which risk treatment?

  • A company decides to stop using a specific third-party software because it has too many security flaws.
  • A hospital buys cybersecurity insurance to cover potential financial losses from a data breach.
  • A school installs a fence around its perimeter to prevent unauthorized entry.
  • A small business decides not to upgrade its antivirus because the subscription is too expensive and it does not think it will be attacked.

Answer key

Activities 1 and 2

  1. Avoid: stops the activity causing the risk.
  2. Transfer: insurance shifts the loss.
  3. Reduce: a fence lowers the likelihood of entry.
  4. Accept: no action taken.
  1. B: officers recommend and document.
  2. B: reduce is most common and practical.
  3. B: a record for accountability and future decisions.
  4. C: question 2 asks for cheaper options that still protect the asset.
