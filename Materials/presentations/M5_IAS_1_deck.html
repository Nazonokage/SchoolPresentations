Information Assurance and Security · Module 5

Introduction to the Risk Management Framework

A six-step cycle that selects the right controls at the least cost.

Preview and review

A framework, not a regulation

Quick review: risk
The potential loss, damage or destruction of assets. Example: a hacking attempt on a database could cause data loss and financial penalties.
Important distinction
An RMF is a framework, not a regulation. It is a structured guide that helps organizations select the security measures and controls to protect themselves, their employees and their assets.

Today we focus on the first two of the six steps.

Why it matters

Why use the RMF?

The risk
According to Global Risk Reports, cyber attacks consistently rank among the top 10 risks organizations face, alongside natural disasters and accidents.
The answer
A continuous, cyclical process for maximum security of assets at the least cost. It assumes you have already identified your risks.

Continuous reassessment is the heart of RMF: a control perfect yesterday may be obsolete or too expensive tomorrow.

The cycle

Six steps, never ending

  1. 1. Categorize information systems.
  2. 2. Select security controls.
  3. 3. Implement security controls.
  4. 4. Assess security controls.
  5. 5. Authorize information systems.
  6. 6. Monitor security controls, then loop back to step 1.

The risk landscape changes over time, so monitoring leads back to categorizing.

Steps 1 and 2

Categorize, then select

Step 1: Categorize information systems
Create priority levels from least to most important. Assign each risk a priority by its likelihood and severity. A risk matrix comes in the next modules.
Step 2: Select security controls
Choose the best control for every identified risk, by type and by function.
TypeDescriptionExamples
PhysicalTangible barriersGuards, locked doors, fences, CCTV
TechnicalTechnology-basedFirewalls, encryption, antivirus
AdministrativePolicies, proceduresAwareness training, incident response plans
FunctionDescriptionFocus
PreventativeStops an incidentAccess control, fences
DetectiveIdentifies an incidentIntrusion detection, audit logs
CorrectiveFixes or restoresBackups, disaster recovery plans

Types and functions return in Modules 13 and 14.

Steps 3 and 4

Implement, then assess

Step 3: Implement
Apply the selected controls to the identified risks. In practice: technical configuration and policy rollout. In this course: labs and hands-on activities.
Step 4: Assess
Check that the control is truly the best fit. Example: you plan security guards, find them too expensive and switch to CCTV, keeping a human presence only where intruders are captured.
Steps 5 and 6

Authorize, then monitor

Step 5: Authorize
Get executives to approve the controls: formal sign-off, memos, policies and compliance with relevant laws.
Step 6: Monitor
Keep checking the control and the risk. Has likelihood or severity changed? Is the control too expensive or outdated? If yes, the cycle repeats, possibly from step 1.
Analogy

Renovating an old house

  1. Categorize: the kitchen (critical) before the guest room (low).
  2. Select: a new lock (physical) and a smart alarm (technical).
  3. Implement: install them.
  1. Assess: test the locks and alarm; adjust.
  2. Authorize: the homeowner signs off.
  3. Monitor: a year later, check for rust or batteries. If the neighborhood is safer, you might downgrade the alarm (re-categorize).

Questions answered

Frequently asked

Other ways to select controls?
Yes, by categories such as maintenance, planning, media protection and access controls. Type and function is the most general baseline.
Why authorize?
Executives who fund security must know and approve the controls, or you may lack budget, authority or legal backing.
Skip monitoring?
Controls become outdated or ineffective as the risk environment changes.
Only for cybersecurity?
No. It also fits physical security, disaster preparedness and financial risk.

Recap

Key takeaways

  • The RMF is a 6-step cycle that maximizes security while minimizing cost.
  • It is a framework, not a rigid regulation.
  • It is continuous because risks, likelihood and severity are dynamic.
  • Executive authorization secures funding and compliance.
  • Controls are classed by type (physical, technical, administrative) and function (preventative, detective, corrective).
