Module 2
Handheld & Wearable Devices
Core Concepts & Definition
Technological Device
Any computing system, cellular phone, smartphone, digital camera, video camera, audio recording instrument, or specialized electronic architecture engineered to create, store, securely isolate, or transmit electronic data.
Key Capabilities
  • Creation – capture raw inputs → digital metrics
  • Storage – local retention of transactional & telemetry data
  • Transmission – move payloads across distributed networks
Handheld Device Anatomy
  • Display: LCD or OLED frameworks
  • Input: Dual-modality (touchscreens + physical keypads or virtual matrices)
  • Connectivity: Wi‑Fi, Bluetooth, cellular (3G+), NFC
Wearable Systems
Products controlled by integrated electronic components & embedded micro-software engines incorporated into clothing or worn on the body.
  • Smart Glasses (OHMDs)
  • Smartwatches
  • Biosensors · HMDs (AR/VR)
💡 Wearables are like “computing you wear” – from fitness bands to AR glasses.

Module 3
Touch User Interfaces (TUI)
Definition · GUI vs TUI · Design Considerations
What is TUI?
A computer‑pointing technology based upon the sense of touch (haptics).
Accessibility Provides tactile/Braille input – beneficial for users with visual impairments.
GUI vs TUI
  • GUI: relies primarily on sight; windows, icons, menus; indirect input (mouse)
  • TUI: relies on touch; direct physical manipulation
⚡ Mainstream breakthrough: 2007 (Microsoft Surface + Apple iPhone)
Design Considerations Wigdor & Wixon, 2011
  1. Extension of the Body – feels natural
  2. Natural Experience – intuitive, real‑world physics
  3. Immediate Feedback – instant response
  4. Size Factor – ergonomics & finger size
Core Interaction Techniques
  • Tapping‑it – most common; brief touch
  • Crossing‑in – drag across target; aids motor‑impaired
  • Directional Gesturing – no predefined targets; motion‑based
🖐️ TUI is like “touching the interface” – direct, immediate, and natural.
Module 3
Touchscreen Technologies
Resistive · Capacitive · SAW · IR
1. Wire Resistive
How: Pressure forces layers to contact, changing voltage.
Any input (finger, stylus, gloved hand).
Low cost · Low power · Vulnerable to scratches
2. Surface Capacitive
How: Reacts to static electrical capacity of human body.
Skin only (or specialized stylus).
Superior clarity · Durable
3. Projected Capacitive (P‑CAP)
How: 3D electrostatic field via embedded IC chip.
Skin or thin gloves · Multi‑touch.
Excellent clarity · Scratch‑resistant
4. Surface Acoustic Wave (SAW)
How: Ultrasonic waves; absorption maps touch.
Bare finger or soft glove · Multi‑touch.
Excellent clarity · Sensitive to contaminants
5. Infrared (IR) Touchscreen
How: IR emitters/sensors create light grid; interruption maps location.
Highest clarity & light transmission · Infinite durability · Any object that blocks beams.
Higher cost · Sensitive to water droplets, ambient light

Auditory Interfaces & Sonification
Why sound? · The Processing Loop
Why Auditory Interfaces?
  • Complement visual interfaces
  • Reduce visual overload
  • Support tasks where sight is limited
  • Enable multitasking (e.g., driving)
  • Provide situational awareness
Auditory Interface Definition
A bidirectional, communicative connection between human and product.
  • Machine side: listening, speech recognition, dialogue
  • Human side: auditory displays (sounds → information)
The Processing Loop
DATA → A (Representation) → B (Application Loop) → C (Sonification/Rendering) → D (Sound Display) → USER
↑ ↓
└──────────────────── INTERACTIONS ─────────────────────┘
Module 4
4 Types of Auditory Interfaces
1. Audification
Direct translation of a data waveform to the audible domain.
Example: Seismic data sped up – vibrations become rumble/roar.
Works best with large datasets with periodic features.
2. Sonification
Use of non‑speech audio to convey information or perceptualize data.
Example: Geiger counter – faster clicks = higher radiation.
Auditory perception excels at temporal, spatial, amplitude, frequency.
3. Earcon
Brief, distinctive sound representing a specific event.
Coined by D.A. Sumikawa (1985) – pun on “icon”.
Examples: OS error beep, startup/shutdown sounds, NBC three‑tone chime.
4. Voicemail System
Computer‑based system for exchanging personal voice messages.
Also known as: voice message or voice bank.
Example: *98 Vertical Service Code (US landlines).
🔊 Auditory interfaces are like “sound as data” – each type tells a different story.

Module 5
QR Codes (Quick Response)
Definition · History · Barcode vs QR
What is a QR Code?
A 2D matrix barcode that stores data in a grid of modules.
Invented in 1994 by Denso Wave (Toyota subsidiary) for vehicle/parts tracking.
Approved as ISO international standard in 2000.
1D Barcode vs QR Code
  • 1D Barcode: parallel lines, read left→right, small data (ID number)
  • QR Code: 2D grid, any direction, large data (Kanji, alphanumeric)
📖 Analogy: 1D = a sentence · QR = a paragraph
QR Variants
  • Micro QR – compact, 11×11, up to 21 chars
  • Model 1 – original, ~707 chars max
  • Model 2 – improved, 1–40 versions, 4,296 chars
  • IQR – square/rectangular, 61 formats
  • SQRC – secret function, password‑locked
  • Frame QR – customizable center area
“A set of camera lenses” – same base, different job.
Anatomy of a QR Code
  • Finder Patterns – 3 corner squares for orientation
  • Data Modules – scattered bits; position, not color, carries info
  • Quiet Zone – outer safety border (min 4 modules)
  • Frame/Logo Area – reserved center for branding (Frame QR)

Module 6
Visual Sensation · Perception · Cognition
“A camera pipeline”
The Visual Pathway
Light Stimulus → SENSATION (eye/retina) → PERCEPTION (brain) → COGNITION (memory & action)
Analogy: Sensation = sensor capturing raw light; Perception = image processor recognizing a face; Cognition = deciding to smile or wave.
Sensation vs Perception
  • Sensation: physical experience of light; same for all sighted individuals
  • Perception: mental recognition of colors, patterns; highly subjective
📷 “Camera sensor vs. photo editor” – raw capture vs. interpretation.
How the Eye Works
  • Lens refracts light onto rods & cones (photoreceptors)
  • Rods/cones convert light photons → neural impulses → brain
  • Brightness adaptation: eye cannot transition instantly between extreme dark and light
Pupil = aperture · Lens = focuses · Retina = sensor
Cognition
The mental engine that drives action; transforms perceived information into meaningful action.
  • Basic: attention, awareness, orientation
  • Complex: memory, language, reasoning, problem‑solving, decision‑making

Module 7
Itertools Chunking
The grouper() Recipe
The Problem
Need to process data in fixed‑size batches (chunks) for:
  • API pagination
  • Memory‑limited environments
  • Parallel processing
  • Rate‑limited APIs
Key Features
  • Memory‑efficient – processes one chunk at a time
  • Predictable batches
  • Lazy evaluation – only consumes as iterated
  • Works with any iterable
grouper() Implementation
import itertools as it

                                        def grouper(inputs, n, fillvalue=None):
                                        iters = [iter(inputs)] * n
                                        return it.zip_longest(*iters, fillvalue=fillvalue)

                                        alpha = ['a', 'b', 'c', 'd', 'e', 'f', 'g', 'h', 'i', 'j', 'k', 'l', 'm']
                                        chunks = list(grouper(alpha, 3))
                                        # [('a','b','c'), ('d','e','f'), ... ('m', None, None)]
Creates n references to the same iterator; pads with fillvalue.
🍕 “Eating a pizza slice by slice” – one chunk at a time.
